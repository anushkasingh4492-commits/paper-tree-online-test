Imagine a cinema that sells popcorn in two sizes: a small tub at a modest price and a large tub at nearly twice that. Most customers buy the small one, until the cinema adds a third option, a medium tub priced only slightly below the large. Hardly anyone buys the medium, yet sales of the large tub rise sharply, so that the new option has done its work without ever being chosen. It exists to make the large tub look like a bargain, and in many such cases it succeeds; marketers call such an option a decoy, and the pattern it produces turns up often enough, with magazine subscriptions, holiday packages and software plans as well as snacks, to deserve a more careful explanation than the claim that customers are easily fooled.

The standard account of choice in economics assumes that a person arrives at a shop with a ranking of the available goods already settled, and that the shop merely reveals it. On that account the decoy should be inert: if I prefer the small tub to the large one, the arrival of a medium tub that I do not want should leave my ranking of the other two untouched. What the decoy suggests instead is that many preferences are not retrieved but assembled on the spot, from the comparisons the menu makes easy. The medium tub is easy to compare with the large, being the same product at almost the same price with visibly less popcorn, whereas the comparison between the small and the large, which requires trading money against quantity, is considerably harder. Consequently, people lean on the easy comparison and let it settle the hard one.

It would be a mistake to conclude that preferences are endlessly pliable, for the effect is strongest when buyers are unsure what they want and have little experience of the product; a regular who knows exactly how much popcorn she can eat is rarely moved. It also weakens when the decoy is so plainly inferior that customers notice the trick, and resentment at being steered can push them the other way. These limits matter, because they show that the decoy exploits a gap in knowledge rather than a defect of reason: constructing a preference out of whatever comparisons are available is a perfectly sensible strategy for someone who lacks better information.

The more interesting lesson concerns the menu itself. We usually treat the set of options as neutral background and the decision as the thing to be explained, but the decoy shows that the background is part of the decision. Whoever arranges the options, whether a cinema, a software firm setting its subscription tiers or a restaurant pricing its wine list, is not merely displaying choices but helping to form them. This does not make every arrangement sinister, since some arrangement is unavoidable, but it does mean that the question of what people chose cannot be separated from the question of what they were shown.

Suppose a dozen firms bid for the right to drill in a tract of seabed whose reserves nobody knows, and that each firm commissions its own survey, and the surveys, being estimates, scatter around the true figure, some too high and some too low. If each firm bids what its own survey says the tract is worth, the contract will go to the firm whose survey was most optimistic, so that winning, in other words, is itself evidence of having overestimated. The firm that celebrates its victory has been selected for its error, and it will on average pay more than the tract is worth. This phenomenon is the winner's curse, and the striking thing about it is that no bidder needs to be foolish for it to occur, since each estimate may be honest and, taken alone, unbiased.

The remedy, in principle, is simple: a bidder should ask not what the tract is worth given its survey, but what it is worth given its survey and given that it has won. The second question yields a lower number, and a bidder who answers it will shade its bid below the raw estimate, more sharply the more rivals there are, since the highest of many estimates is more likely to be inflated than the highest of a few. Shading of this kind is not timidity but the correct response to a feature of the situation that the unconditioned estimate ignores.

A natural objection is that the curse should vanish with experience, because firms that overpay will lose money, notice and adjust, while those that do not will be driven from the market. There is truth in this, and seasoned bidders in many settings do shade their bids, but the learning is slower than the objection supposes. The outcome of any single auction is noisy, since a tract that disappoints may simply have been unlucky. Worse, the firms that lose never discover what the prize was really worth, so the information that would correct everyone's estimates is gathered only by the winners, and slowly. A lesson that must be extracted from a few expensive and ambiguous victories is not quickly learned.

For this reason the design of an auction matters as much as the wisdom of its participants. Formats in which bids are revealed as they rise allow each bidder to watch rivals drop out and to infer something about their estimates, and a bidder who can see how far others are willing to go has less reason to fear that its own enthusiasm is an error. Sealed bids, by contrast, leave each participant alone with a single survey and its uncorrected optimism. The point is not that one format is always superior, since open bidding has weaknesses of its own, among them the ease with which rivals can signal to one another, but that the curse is a property of the information structure, and so can be eased by changing that structure rather than by exhorting bidders to be cleverer.

CAT-VARC-P05-003 · Markets and behaviour · passage Medium · 496 words · 4 questions

A great deal of advertising tells the viewer almost nothing. A television spot in which a famous athlete drinks a soft drink on a beach conveys no fact about the drink's price, ingredients or taste that a sceptical viewer could check, and critics of advertising have long drawn what seems the obvious conclusion: if the message carries no information, the money spent on it is either wasted or devoted to manipulation, persuading people to want things by associating them with glamour. The conclusion is natural, but it rests on the assumption that whatever an advertisement communicates must be contained in what it says or shows, and that assumption is open to question.

Consider the matter from the firm's side. A company launching a product that is cheap to try, such as a breakfast cereal or a shampoo, makes its money not from the first purchase but from the repeat purchases of customers who liked it. A firm confident that its product is good therefore expects a long stream of returns from anyone it persuades to try it once, whereas a firm that knows its product is mediocre expects most of those who try it never to return. Lavish advertising is accordingly worth far more to the first firm than to the second, and a consumer who sees a product advertised extravagantly may reasonably infer that its maker is wagering on repeat business. On this view the expense of the advertisement is itself the message, and the athlete and the beach matter chiefly because everyone can see that they cost a fortune.

The argument has real force, but its reach is limited. It applies most clearly to what economists call experience goods, whose quality can be judged only after use, and only where repeat purchase is likely; a firm selling something that people buy once in a lifetime gains little from the signal, because satisfied customers will not return in any case. For goods whose qualities can be inspected before purchase, such as the dimensions of a table or the fare for a train journey, advertising informs directly and requires no signalling story at all. Nor does the argument show that every extravagant campaign is a reliable signal, since a firm may overspend from vanity or miscalculation, and imitators may learn to mimic the outward appearance of expense more cheaply than it seems.

A familiar objection is that ordinary consumers do not reason in this way, since nobody watching a soft drink advertisement contemplates the economics of repeat purchase. The objection misunderstands what the argument requires. Consumers need not reconstruct the logic; it is enough that they follow a rule of thumb, such as trusting brands they have heard of, and that the rule tends, on the whole, to reward them. The signalling account does not vindicate advertising, and it leaves the question of manipulation entirely open. What it does establish is that an advertisement without content is not, for that reason alone, an advertisement without meaning.

Defenders of nudging, the practice of steering choices by altering how options are presented rather than by forbidding or taxing any of them, have a ready answer to the charge that it is manipulative. Someone, they point out, must decide which option on a form is pre-selected, which dish stands at eye level in a canteen and which paragraph of a letter comes first; since there is no presentation without some arrangement, the choice is not between influencing people and leaving them alone but between influencing them thoughtfully and influencing them carelessly. The argument is sound as far as it goes, and critics who speak as though an unarranged menu were available are attacking a position nobody holds.

Yet the argument from inevitability answers a weaker objection than the one that matters. What troubles the more careful critics is not that choice architects influence us, which is unavoidable, but that many of the most effective nudges work precisely to the extent that they go unnoticed. A default enrolment in a savings scheme succeeds partly because inertia keeps people where they were placed, but also because many never register that a decision was made on their behalf; a smaller plate reduces consumption chiefly when diners do not reflect that they have been served less. Influence of this sort does not engage the faculties by which people govern themselves, but routes around them.

One way to sharpen the worry is to ask whether a given nudge would survive disclosure. Suppose a canteen posted a notice explaining that fruit had been placed at the front because the management wanted employees to eat more of it. If the arrangement continues to work, its power did not depend on concealment, and it begins to look less like manipulation than like a reminder. If it stops working once explained, that is evidence that it relied on a gap between what people were doing and what they understood themselves to be doing, and an institution that relies on such gaps owes its public a justification that it cannot easily give in public.

The test is not decisive in every case, and it should not be mistaken for a general condemnation. Some nudges would plainly survive disclosure: a letter telling people that most of their neighbours pay their taxes on time would plausibly work whether or not recipients knew why it had been sent, since the information is itself the influence. Others might fail it for innocent reasons, because a disclosure can itself distract, or can provoke a reflexive contrariness that has nothing to do with the merits of the option. What the test accomplishes is to shift the debate from an unanswerable question, whether influence is present, to an answerable one, whether the influence could be avowed. That shift concedes to the defenders everything their best argument actually establishes, and withholds from them only what it never established.

When information was scarce, the economic problem it posed was one of supply: how to print, carry and store enough of it for the people who wanted it. That problem has largely been solved, and its solution has exposed another. The abundance of material has made scarce the one resource that every piece of it requires, namely the attention of those who are meant to receive it. Every article, message and video competes for the same limited hours of the same limited minds, and while the cost of producing one more piece of content has fallen almost to nothing, the supply of attention has not grown at all.

This shift explains a feature of modern commerce that once seemed puzzling, the enormous number of services offered at no charge. A search engine, a social network or a mobile game that costs its user nothing is not a gift; it is paid for with attention, which the provider gathers and resells to advertisers, so that the user is less a customer than a supplier of the commodity being traded. Described in this way, the arrangement is not obviously unfair. Many people would gladly exchange a few seconds of distraction for a useful map or a convenient way of keeping in touch with distant friends, and for them the bargain may be a good one.

The difficulty lies elsewhere, in the fact that the price is never stated. When a product is sold for money, its price is a number that buyers can compare across sellers, and the comparison disciplines firms, since a shop that charges too much loses its customers to a cheaper rival. Attention has no such number attached to it. A user cannot easily tell whether one free application takes ten minutes of her day or two hours, still less whether a rival would take less, and so the competitive pressure that keeps money prices in check operates feebly, if at all. Firms competing for attention are therefore rewarded not for charging less of it but for extracting more, and the design of their products follows accordingly, towards endless feeds, automatic playback and notifications timed to recapture a wandering mind.

It does not follow that free services should be prohibited or that their users are dupes. What follows is narrower and, in a way, more practical: a market in which one side cannot see the price it pays will not protect that side as markets ordinarily do, and remedies should therefore aim at making the price visible. A service that reported to each user, plainly and regularly, how much time it had absorbed would not end the trade in attention, but it would give its users what every functioning market requires, a figure to compare.

In most markets the price does the work of allocation. If more people want a good than there are units of it, the price rises until the excess disappears, and whoever is willing to pay the new price obtains the good. But there is a family of markets in which this mechanism cannot operate, because each side must choose the other. A university cannot simply admit whoever is willing to pay the most, nor can an applicant simply buy a place; a hospital hiring junior doctors cares which doctors it gets, and the doctors care which hospital they join. In such markets, sometimes called matching markets, a transaction requires mutual acceptance, and no single number can bring about a match that both sides want.

Left to themselves, matching markets tend to fail in characteristic ways. One is unravelling: because the strongest candidates are in demand, employers try to secure them by making offers earlier than their rivals, and the competition for earliness can push offers so far forward that they are made before anyone knows how good the candidates are. Another is congestion: when offers must be accepted or refused quickly, a candidate holding one offer may not learn in time of a better one, and the market clears without having considered all its possibilities. A third is strategic misrepresentation, in which participants conceal their true preferences, ranking a likely option first rather than the one they actually want, because they fear that revealing their real wishes will cost them.

The remedy that has often been adopted is not deregulation but construction: a clearinghouse to which both sides submit ranked lists, and which computes an assignment by a procedure known to have certain properties. The property most often sought is stability, meaning that there is no applicant and employer, not matched to each other, who would both prefer to be. A stable assignment removes the motive to break ranks, since no such pair can do better by striking a private deal, and with a suitable procedure at least one side can be given no reason to misreport its preferences. Such clearinghouses show that a market is not always a spontaneous order arising wherever people are free to trade; it can be an artefact, built to solve problems that freedom alone creates.

None of this means that design can substitute for the willingness of participants. A clearinghouse works only if enough of both sides join it, and an elegant procedure is useless if the most sought-after employers find it more profitable to recruit privately. Nor can a procedure deliver every desirable property at once: no procedure, for instance, can give both sides complete reason to report honestly while always producing stable outcomes. The designer chooses among imperfect options, and the choice reflects judgements about whose interests deserve protection. The engineering of markets is thus a political as well as a technical art, but it is an art that can be practised well or badly, and the difference matters to the people whose lives it arranges.

For a long time economists were embarrassed by a simple question. Water is essential to life and diamonds are not, yet water is cheap and diamonds are dear; how could prices, which were supposed to reflect value, so plainly invert the order of importance? Eighteenth-century writers posed the puzzle as a contrast between value in use and value in exchange, and many concluded that exchange value must therefore have some source other than usefulness. The most influential answer located that source in the costs of production, and above all in labour: diamonds are dear because finding and cutting them takes enormous effort, while water, in most places, merely has to be collected.

The cost theory had obvious merits, since goods that are hard to produce are frequently expensive, but it struggled with cases in which effort and price come apart. A pearl found by chance in an oyster served at dinner fetches no less than one obtained by days of diving, and a wine from a celebrated year commands its price long after the labour that made it has been forgotten. The resolution that eventually prevailed, worked out independently by several writers in the later nineteenth century, abandoned the attempt to attach value to goods as such. What matters, it held, is not the usefulness of water in general but the usefulness of one more unit of it to someone who already has a certain amount. A person with enough water to drink, cook and wash would gain little from an extra bucket, while a person with few diamonds, or none, would prize one more highly. Price tracks this marginal usefulness, not the total.

The resolution is genuine, and its elegance is rightly admired. But it achieved both more than it is usually credited with and less. It achieved more because it moved value from things to situations: the same bucket of water is nearly worthless in a town beside a river and beyond price to a traveller lost in a desert, although nothing about the water has changed. It achieved less because it was never a theory of importance. To say that water is cheap is to say that, where it is plentiful, nobody would give much for one more unit of it; it is not to say that water matters little. The marginal account explains why prices may be low on goods that are indispensable, and in doing so it warns us against reading prices as a ranking of what matters.

That warning is regularly forgotten. Public argument often treats the market price of something as the measure of its contribution, so that an activity commanding high prices is assumed to be more valuable to society than one commanding low ones, and resources that cost nothing, such as clean air, are treated as though they were of no account until they become scarce. The marginal theory does not license this inference; if anything it predicts the error, since it shows that abundance alone can drive a price towards zero whatever the total importance of the good. Abundance, moreover, is not always permanent, and a price set while a resource was plentiful says little about what it will be worth once it is not.

None of this is a case against prices, which remain an unrivalled means of coordinating what millions of people do with information that no single mind possesses. It is a case for remembering what question a price answers. A price tells us what one more unit of something is worth to those at the margin, here and now. Give a group of people an ordinary object, a coffee mug, say, or a pen, and then ask them how much they would accept to sell it; ask another group, who were not given the object, how much they would pay to acquire one. In experiments of this kind the sellers' prices have repeatedly been found to exceed the buyers' by a wide margin, even though the two groups were formed at random and there is no reason to think that the owners care more for mugs. Merely owning a thing, it seems, makes it more valuable to its owner. The finding has come to be known as the endowment effect, and it has become one of the best-known results in behavioural economics.

The standard interpretation explains it by loss aversion. People, on this view, evaluate outcomes not in absolute terms but as gains and losses relative to a reference point, and losses loom larger than equivalent gains. Once the mug is mine, giving it up registers as a loss, whereas acquiring it, for someone who does not own it, registers only as a gain, and since the loss weighs more heavily, the owner demands more in compensation than the non-owner will pay. The account is economical, and it connects the mug experiments with a wide range of other behaviour, from the reluctance of investors to sell shares that have fallen in value to the stubbornness with which people defend entitlements they have only recently acquired.

A rival interpretation treats the gap as, at least in part, an artefact of the way such experiments are conducted. Participants who have never sold anything in a laboratory may be unsure what is being asked, may suspect that the experimenter knows the object's true value, or may simply apply a rule from everyday life, never to sell for less than it would cost to replace the thing. When procedures are altered so that participants practise the bidding mechanism and understand that misstating their values cannot help them, some studies have found the gap to shrink considerably. Supporters of this view also point out that professional traders, who buy and sell as a matter of routine, appear to show much less of the effect in the goods they deal in.

Each interpretation captures something real, and the dispute is less sharp than it is sometimes made to appear. The traders' case is telling not because it refutes loss aversion but because it suggests where loss aversion applies. A dealer who holds a stock of mugs holds them in order to sell them; parting with one is the purpose of having it, and so it does not register as a loss. A person who has been given a mug to use is in a different position, because the mug has already become, in a small way, part of her possessions, and parting with it means giving something up rather than completing a transaction. On this reading, what matters is not ownership as such but the frame in which a thing is held, as something to use or as something to exchange.

That reading has consequences beyond the laboratory. It implies that the gap between buying and selling prices is not a fixed constant of human nature, to be entered into economic models as a permanent correction, but a variable that depends on how people come to think of what they have. Markets that encourage people to regard their possessions as stock, as resale platforms arguably do, may weaken the effect; arrangements that encourage people to regard what they hold as their own, as long trial periods for products arguably do, may strengthen it. Whether either tendency is desirable is a separate question. The point is that the endowment effect, properly understood, is less a fact about objects or owners than a fact about the relation between them.

A buyer who pays the price only because he expects someone else to pay more, and who would not hold the asset on its own merits for a day, is taking part in something of a different kind: a chain whose value depends on recruiting a next buyer, and which must at some point break.

The difficulty is that this distinguishing mark lies in motives, and prices do not record motives. Two buyers paying the same price on the same day may be doing quite different things, and an observer who sees only the price cannot tell how much of a market consists of believers and how much of passers-on. That is why proposals that regulators should identify bubbles as they form and deflate them, by raising interest rates or restricting purchases, ask for a judgement that is rarely available in time, and why such measures, if applied, are likely to fall on sound and unsound investments alike.

A more modest aim is within reach. What turns the collapse of a price into a wider calamity is usually not the fall itself but the borrowing that financed the purchases, because losses on borrowed money pass from the buyer to the lender, and from the lender to everyone who depends on it. Limits on leverage do not require anyone to know whether a price is justified; they require only that whoever turns out to be wrong should be wrong mainly with his own money. This is less dramatic than a promise to prick bubbles before they grow, and it will disappoint those who want the next crash prevented rather than contained, but a rule that works without foresight has the considerable merit of not depending on a capacity that no one possesses. Such a policy does not aim to prevent bubbles. It aims to make them survivable.

The textbook account of banking runs roughly as follows. Savers deposit money; a bank keeps a fraction of each deposit as reserves and lends out the rest; the borrower spends the loan, the recipient deposits it at another bank, which in turn lends out most of it, and so on, until the original deposit has been multiplied by a factor that depends on the fraction held back. As a description of what actually happens when a loan is made, this has the sequence backwards. A bank that approves a loan does not hand over notes taken from a vault of accumulated deposits; it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit, and the money the borrower then spends did not exist a moment earlier. Lending creates deposits rather than the reverse. What limits a lender is therefore not a pool of prior savings but its judgement of whether a loan will be repaid at a profit, the capital it must hold against possible losses, the demand from creditworthy borrowers, and the price at which it can obtain the reserves needed to settle payments with other banks, a price that the central bank sets through the rate of interest rather than through the quantity of reserves.

The textbook is not simply wrong. A single bank that lends freely will soon find its new deposits draining away as borrowers pay people who bank elsewhere, and it must then attract deposits or borrow reserves to cover the outflow; from the standpoint of one institution, lending does seem to require funding. The error lies in generalising from the single bank to the system. What is a drain for one bank is an inflow for another, and the system as a whole is constrained less by the deposits it has gathered than by the willingness of banks to lend and of borrowers to borrow.

The difference matters for policy. If the multiplier story were right, a central bank that supplied banks with abundant reserves would set off a roughly proportionate expansion of lending. Arguments about why lending has stalled are then better directed at the demand for credit and the condition of banks' balance sheets than at the quantity of reserves.

A further implication is drawn less often. If instead banks create money when they lend, then their decisions about whom to lend to determine what new purchasing power enters the economy, and where. A boom in lending against existing houses and a boom in lending to firms that build new things both enlarge the supply of money, yet they do very different things with it: the first mainly raises the price of what is already there, while the second adds to what there is. Few ideas in economics are cited as often, or misunderstood as thoroughly, as comparative advantage. In everyday commentary trade is described as a contest: a country wins when it is better at making things than its rivals and loses when they overtake it. On this view a nation that is less productive than its neighbours in every industry would have nothing to sell and would be wise to close its borders. The principle of comparative advantage was formulated precisely to show why this conclusion does not follow, and the fact that the conclusion is still drawn suggests that the principle is more often invoked than understood.

The core insight concerns opportunity cost rather than absolute ability. Consider a hypothetical lawyer who types faster than any secretary she could hire. It does not follow that she should do her own typing, because every hour spent at the keyboard is an hour withdrawn from legal work that earns far more. The secretary, though slower at typing, gives up much less by doing it. Both are better off when each concentrates on the task in which his or her relative sacrifice is smallest and they exchange the results. The same reasoning applies to countries. What determines the pattern of trade is not which country is best at producing a good, but which country forgoes the least of other goods in producing it, and every country, however unproductive overall, has some activity in which that sacrifice is comparatively low.

Two consequences follow that sit uneasily with the language of contest. First, gains from trade do not require one party to lose: the lawyer and the secretary both gain, and so do a rich and a poor economy that trade on the basis of their differing costs. Second, a country cannot have a comparative advantage in everything, since being relatively better at one activity means, by definition, being relatively worse at another. A government that promises to make its nation competitive in every industry is therefore promising something that is not merely difficult but incoherent.

None of this implies that the critics of open trade have nothing to say. The textbook argument compares two settled states and is silent about the passage between them; workers who lose their jobs when an industry contracts do not costlessly reappear in the industry that expands, and the losses can be concentrated in particular towns for a generation. Comparative advantage can also shift over time as skills and capital accumulate, which is why some economists defend temporary support for new industries. These are serious objections, but they are objections to the timing and distribution of the gains, not to the logic of opportunity cost. They do not restore the image of trade as a race in which one runner's progress is another's defeat.

When a product is made from start to finish in a single country, this convention is harmless. When it is assembled from parts made in a dozen, the convention produces a curious distortion: the country where the last screw is turned is credited with the entire value of the product, including the much larger share that was created elsewhere and merely passed through.

Consider a hypothetical tablet computer assembled in one country from a display made in a second, chips designed in a third and fabricated in a fourth, and software written in a fifth. When the finished tablet is shipped to a sixth country, the statistics record an export of its full factory price from the assembling country, though the work done there may account for only a small fraction of that price. The bilateral balance between the assembler and the buyer swells accordingly, while the suppliers of the costlier components, whose contribution travels unseen inside the assembler's shipment, appear in the bilateral figures as having sold nothing to the final market at all.

Statisticians have responded by trying to trace value added: to ask, for each product, how much of its value was created in each country, and to tally trade on that basis. The striking feature of the results is not that a country's overall position changes, for it largely does not. Its balance with the world as a whole is much the same however one counts, since the value of each imported component is recorded once as an import and again inside the export, and the two entries cancel. What changes is the map: the bilateral balances that attract political attention are redistributed, often substantially, from assemblers towards the countries that supply the most valuable parts.

This matters because bilateral balances are routinely read as a scorecard of who is gaining at whose expense, and policy is sometimes made on that reading. A tariff aimed at the country with which a large deficit is recorded may, on the value-added view, fall chiefly on workers who contributed a modest share of what was bought, while leaving untouched the countries that contributed the most. The value-added figures are not beyond dispute. They depend on tables of who buys inputs from whom that are compiled infrequently and filled out with estimates, and they cannot replace the gross figures that customs administrations need for their own purposes. But one need not regard them as precise to see that they expose a habit of reading the gross bilateral figures as though they measured something they were never built to measure.

Its premise is that productivity in many industries is learned rather than given. A new steelworks or shipyard in a country without one begins with inexperienced workers, untested managers and suppliers who do not yet know what it needs; it will make costly mistakes for years before it reaches the efficiency of established foreign rivals. If it must meet those rivals at once, it may never survive long enough to learn. A period of protection, on this view, is not a subsidy to inefficiency but an investment in the learning that will eventually remove it.

The argument gains force from a further point. Much of what a pioneering firm learns does not stay inside it. Workers it trains move to competitors, suppliers it develops go on to serve other customers, and the mere demonstration that an industry can work in a given place lowers the risk for those who follow. Over time a cluster of related firms may form around the pioneer, each drawing on skills that the pioneer paid to create. Because the pioneer bears the cost of this learning but captures only part of its benefit, an unaided market will produce too little of it, and there is a case in principle for public support.

The difficulty lies not in the logic but in the ending. Protection is granted on the promise that it is temporary, yet every year it lasts it builds a constituency for its continuation: owners whose profits depend on it, workers whose jobs appear to, and officials whose standing is tied to the industry's survival. Worse, the protection itself weakens the motive to learn. A firm that knows its shelter will be extended whenever it fails to become competitive has little reason to bear the pain of becoming so, and the infant can remain an infant indefinitely, growing larger without growing up. The many cases in which protection outlived any plausible learning period are not accidents of bad administration; they are what the incentives predict.

It follows that the argument stands or falls on whether the exit can be made credible in advance. Protection whose duration is fixed and public, whose renewal depends on measurable progress, and whose beneficiaries are required to compete in some market where the shelter does not reach them, such as export markets, is a different policy from protection granted open-endedly. Export performance is especially revealing, because a firm cannot fake competitiveness in a market it does not control. A protected firm that must also sell abroad, at prices set by rivals it cannot shut out, learns quickly whether it has improved, and so does the government that shelters it. The interesting historical question is therefore not whether successful industrialisers protected their industries, since many did, but whether they attached conditions that forced the protected firms to improve.

None of this makes the conditions easy to impose. Withdrawing support from a large employer that has missed its targets requires a government able to bear the political cost, and the very constituency that protection creates is what makes that cost high. Protection also has victims who are easy to overlook: the firms that must buy the sheltered product at a higher price, and whose own competitiveness suffers as a result. The infant-industry case, then, is best understood not as a general licence but as a demanding bargain, sound in theory and available in practice only to states that can enforce its terms.

An investor asked in advance would have no better basis for a forecast than an official; both would be guessing. Producing it is costly, and here the market's usual advantages turn against it. Officials who meet the same managers every month will find it hard to tell them, when a review date arrives, that support is ending. That is a weaker guarantee than the advocates of industrial policy would like, but it is a stronger one than its critics usually assume.

One camp prefers a tax: the government announces a charge per tonne of emissions and leaves firms to decide how much to cut. Economists call this leakage, and fear of it has been among the most effective arguments against acting alone. The obvious answer is a border adjustment: a charge on imports equal to the carbon price that would have been paid had the goods been made at home, perhaps paired with a rebate for exporters. The proposal is simpler to state than to carry out.
