Archives were not assembled to answer the questions historians now put to them. A colonial archive records what an administration needed to know: revenue collected and revenue owed, disorders suppressed, petitions received and disposed of. The people it governed appear in its pages chiefly at the moments when they became a problem for the state, as defaulters, suspects, rebels or supplicants. A historian who reads such records along the grain, accepting their categories as descriptions of reality, will end up reproducing the administration's own view of the world, in which a refusal to pay becomes 'obstinacy' and a gathering at a shrine becomes a 'disturbance of the peace'.

The remedy most often proposed is to read against the grain: to treat the record as evidence of what it was designed to suppress or dismiss. A report that a village had refused its dues 'without any cause' can be read for the cause the clerk declined to set down. The insistence of officials, repeated across a dozen letters, that a rumour was baseless tells us that the rumour mattered a great deal to them, and probably to those who spread it. Fear, in such documents, is a kind of evidence. Practised with care, this method has produced some of the most illuminating social history of recent generations, restoring motive and intelligence to people whom the record had reduced to nuisances.

Yet the method has a limit that its enthusiasts sometimes forget. Reading against the grain still reads the grain. The voices it recovers belong to those who left a trace by colliding with power; those who never collided, who paid what was asked, married, worked their fields and died without incident, remain exactly where the archive left them, outside it. There is also a quieter temptation. When the record is thin, the historian's sympathies rush in to fill it, and the rebel recovered from the margins of an official report can look suspiciously like the rebel the historian set out hoping to find.

None of this is a reason to abandon the method. It is a reason to be candid about what it can and cannot do. A silence in the archive is not always a hidden voice waiting to be decoded; sometimes it is simply an absence, and the honest response is to mark it as one. Beyond that, the historian can turn to sources that were not produced by the same gaze: remembered songs and stories, the remains of houses and tools, the shape of fields and paths. The archive can be cross-examined with great profit, but it cannot be made to testify about what it never saw.

Arguments about public statues tend to be conducted as if there were only two positions. On one side are those who say that the statue of a figure now held in disrepute should come down, because a city ought not to go on honouring what it has come to find shameful. On the other are those who say that removing it 'erases history', and that a mature society learns from its past instead of hiding it. The quarrel is loud, and it is often bitter, because each side suspects the other of bad faith: of wishing to rewrite the past, or of wishing to keep its injustices on a pedestal. Each side assumes it knows what a statue is. Neither, I think, quite does.

A statue is not a history lesson. It records almost nothing about the life of its subject: a name, a pair of dates, perhaps a line of praise. What it records very precisely is a decision, taken at a particular moment by particular people, to honour that subject in that place. Many of the statues now in dispute were raised long after the events they commemorate, often by committees with purposes of their own, such as asserting the standing of a group or a cause at a time when that standing was being questioned. A statue is therefore better evidence about the people who raised it than about the person it depicts.

Seen in this light, the charge of erasing history loses much of its force. Taking a statue down does not alter what happened, and the books, records and family memories that tell its subject's story remain where they were. What removal changes is a present act of honour, and every generation is entitled to reconsider whom it honours. Yet the other camp should not feel wholly vindicated. A statue that is quietly carted away also removes evidence of what an earlier generation chose to celebrate, and that evidence can be uncomfortable in useful ways, reminding a city that the values it now rejects were once publicly and confidently held by respectable people.

The popular compromise of leaving the statue in place and adding an explanatory plaque is often presented as the neutral option. It is not. A plaque is also an editorial act: someone must decide what it says, how long it is and whose judgement it expresses, and a few lines of small print beneath a towering bronze figure rarely win the argument with it. Moving a statue to a museum, where the date and purpose of its making can be displayed alongside it, may come closer to keeping the evidence without continuing the honour. Whatever a community decides, it should at least be clear that it is deciding how to honour, not whether to remember.

CAT-VARC-P04-003 · History and memory · passage Medium · 444 words · 4 questions

Most national histories begin with a land and a people presented as though they had always been there, waiting to become a nation. Kings who reigned many centuries earlier, battles fought for reasons that had nothing to do with any modern state, and poets who wrote in dialects their supposed descendants can barely read are enlisted as the opening chapters of a single story whose last chapter is the present. Rulers who owed allegiance to dynasties spread across several of today's countries, and who would not have recognised the modern map, are recast as founders. The past is read backwards from its outcome and then retold forwards as if the outcome had been its purpose all along.

Critics call this anachronism, and on the main charge they are right. But the criticism is often pressed further than it can go, into the claim that because nations are modern creations, the materials of national history are simply invented. That does not follow. Languages, forms of worship, routes of trade and shared songs long predated modern states, and they plainly did shape which communities later found it easy, and which found it hard, to imagine themselves as one. The national historian's mistake is not to notice these continuities. It is to arrange them as stages on the way to an end that was fixed in advance.

The distinction matters because the arrangement has consequences. If the nation is the hero of the story, then whatever the past contained that does not fit the hero's career must be either left out or cast as a failure of loyalty. Regions that resisted unification, communities whose ties ran across the eventual border, rival projects that might have produced a different state or several smaller ones: all of these become obstacles overcome rather than possibilities that once existed and were, for a time, taken seriously by the people who lived among them. A province that held out for its own arrangements appears only as a delay, and a language that lost out to the standard tongue appears, if at all, as a quaint survival. The coherence of the story is purchased by narrowing its cast.

A better history would keep the continuities and restore the alternatives. It would ask what other political communities were possible at each point, what the people of the time thought they were choosing between, and why this one prevailed, treating the result as an outcome to be explained rather than a destiny to be celebrated. Such a history may make for less stirring lessons in school. It is also far less likely to make citizens strangers to whole regions and peoples of their own past.

Historians trained on written records have long treated oral testimony with suspicion, and their reasons are not foolish. Memories fade and are reshaped by everything that happened afterwards; a speaker recalling events of fifty years ago has also absorbed fifty years of conversation, newspapers and family retelling, and cannot always tell what she saw from what she later heard. Anyone who has compared an elderly person's account of a local event with the surviving paperwork knows how often the dates, the sequence and even the participants differ. As a chronicle of what happened and when, the spoken memory of a single witness is frequently unreliable.

The objection assumes, however, that the only purpose of testimony is to establish what happened. Consider a hypothetical mining town in which many older residents, interviewed separately, place a bitter strike in the same year as a disastrous flood, although the records show that the two events were several years apart. As evidence about dates, these accounts are simply wrong. As evidence about how the town came to understand its own past, they are remarkably revealing, for the merging of the two events suggests that residents experienced them as a single catastrophe, perhaps because in both the company that owned the mine was felt to have abandoned them.

The crucial point is that shared and patterned errors are not the same as noise. Individual slips of memory are scattered more or less at random; one witness misplaces a year, another confuses two neighbours. When many people independently make the same mistake, however, the mistake becomes a social fact, something that calls for explanation rather than mere correction. And patterned error of this kind is precisely what written records cannot supply, because documents tend to record events, while the meanings that people attached to those events, and the way those meanings settled over decades, rarely find their way into an official file.

None of this licenses the historian to treat every inaccuracy as meaningful or to abandon the ordinary work of checking. An error can be interpreted only once it has been established that it is an error, and that usually requires the very documents that oral historians are sometimes too quick to dismiss. The strongest work therefore uses the two kinds of source together, letting each expose what the other cannot see: the record correcting the memory, and the memory revealing what the record left out.

Many historians regard the question 'what if?' as a parlour game, an amusement for the armchair strategist that has no place in serious scholarship. The past, they say, happened only one way, and the historian's task is to explain that way, not to speculate about others for which no evidence could ever exist. The objection has the appeal of modesty, but it rests on a misunderstanding of what historians already do. To say that a harvest failure caused a revolt is to say, at the very least, that without the failure the revolt would not have occurred when and as it did, or would have been less likely. Every causal claim carries such a counterfactual inside it, and the historian who refuses to examine counterfactuals has not avoided them; he has merely declined to inspect the ones he is already using.

The real question, then, is not whether to reason counterfactually but how to do it with discipline. Two constraints seem essential. The first is that the altered antecedent should be minimal and plausible in its own time: the most useful alternatives are those that people at the moment actually contemplated, such as a decision that was debated and narrowly taken, rather than those that require rewriting the character of a ruler or the technology of an age. The second is that the horizon of consequences should be short. Each step away from the altered moment multiplies the contingencies in play, and within a few years the alternative world becomes as indeterminate as the future, which is why the popular genre of imagining a whole century transformed by one changed battle is closer to fiction than to history.

A more searching objection holds that even a disciplined counterfactual smuggles in assumptions about how societies behave, about which pressures are strong and which choices are free, that cannot themselves be tested. This is true, but it is not peculiar to counterfactuals. The ordinary causal explanation rests on exactly the same assumptions; it simply leaves them unstated. What the explicit counterfactual offers is not freedom from such assumptions but exposure of them, so that a reader can see what the explanation depends on and dispute it.

Used this way, the counterfactual is less a tool for discovering what would have happened than a tool for testing what is claimed to have been bound to happen. Historians and politicians alike are fond of declaring outcomes inevitable. To show that a serious alternative was open, contemplated and within reach at the time is enough to defeat that claim, even when nothing reliable can be said about where the alternative would have led.

The names we give to periods of the past look like neutral conveniences, markers on a timeline that allow us to say roughly when something happened. In fact most of them are compressed arguments. 'The Middle Ages' is the clearest case. The phrase took hold among European writers who believed that they were reviving the learning of the ancient world after a long interruption, and who therefore saw the intervening thousand years as an interval, a middle that mattered chiefly as the gap between two moments of achievement. A period named in this way has been judged before it has been studied.

Generations of historians have since shown how little that judgement fits the evidence. The centuries in question saw the founding of universities, the building of great cathedrals, the elaboration of law and the slow growth of towns and trade; far from being an interruption, they laid much of the ground on which later achievements stood. Yet the name has survived the demolition of the view that produced it, and it continues to whisper that the period was something to be got through. Words outlast the arguments that coined them.

The difficulty grows when such a label is exported. To speak of a 'medieval' period in the history of a region far from Europe is to import a chronology built for one part of the world, with its own sense of what came before and after, and to invite the historian to look there for whatever the label suggests, such as a decline and a later rebirth, whether or not the evidence of that region points to anything of the kind.

It does not follow that we should try to do without period names. History cannot be written without some way of grouping years together, and the alternatives are not innocent either: centuries are as arbitrary as any label, cutting through lives and movements at points chosen by arithmetic, and they bring a peculiar habit of their own, the tendency to treat each hundred years as having a single character. The sensible course is to keep the names while holding them lightly, treating each as a hypothesis about what a stretch of time had in common, to be tested against the evidence and abandoned where it fails. The danger lies not in having labels but in letting the label arrive before the research and decide what the research will look for.

Among professional historians it has become something of a reflex to sneer at heritage. The restored cottage with its costumed guide, the festival that re-enacts a battle every summer, the gift shop selling replica coins and the television drama in which everyone has perfect teeth are all, on this view, so many falsifications: a past made comfortable, sold by the ticket and stripped of everything that might disturb a family on a day out. The critics are not wrong about the facts. Heritage does select, simplify and flatter, and it usually offers the past with the smell taken out.

What the critique misses is that heritage is not attempting to be history and failing at it. The two draw on the same materials, the same buildings, objects and stories, but they put them to different uses. History asks questions of the past and follows the evidence wherever it leads, including towards conclusions that unsettle the people who hear them. Heritage asks the past to confirm something about the present: that this town has deep roots, that this people has endured, that the place where one lives is worth caring about. Its characteristic question is not 'what happened here, and why?' but 'why should this place and its people matter to us?', and its characteristic answer is an experience rather than an argument. It is closer to commemoration than to inquiry, and it should be judged as commemoration is judged.

Consider a family that marks a grandparent's long marriage with a party, old photographs on the walls and affectionate speeches. Nobody expects the speeches to include an account of the couple's worst quarrels, and nobody thinks the omission a lie; the occasion exists to celebrate, and everyone present understands this. The tidying that heritage performs is, in the same way, not itself the offence. A restored village that shows thatched roofs and spinning wheels but not the open drains or the cramped rooms where servants slept is doing what celebrations do. There is even a case that it does history a service, for many people first sense that the past was inhabited by real people when they stand in such a room, and the curiosity awakened there leads some of them on to the harder questions that history asks.

The trouble begins at a specific point, and it is worth locating precisely. It comes when heritage claims the authority of history: when a site presents its selective story not as a celebration but as an account of how things really were, when a guidebook dismisses awkward evidence as the invention of cynics, or when public money that once sustained archives and libraries is redirected to attractions that will draw larger crowds. A museum label that calls a disputed episode 'settled', or a re-enactment whose script quietly drops the side that lost, does more than simplify; it borrows the standing of scholarship in order to close a question that scholarship has left open. At that point celebration has stopped being one use of the past among others and has begun to crowd out the use that tests it.

The sensible relation between the two is neither contempt nor merger. History should remain the critic of heritage, ready to point out what a display leaves out and to say so in public; heritage can remain what it is, a doorway through which many people first enter the past. The line that needs defending is not the one between the professional and the popular, but the one between celebration that knows itself to be celebration and celebration that presents itself as knowledge.

The doctrine that a territory could be treated as belonging to no one, terra nullius, is often described as a lie that settlers told themselves. The lands of Australia or North America, the argument runs, were plainly inhabited; to declare them empty was either a culpable ignorance or a convenient fiction, and once the facts are acknowledged the doctrine collapses. There is something right in this, but it misdescribes the doctrine's real strength. Most of the people who invoked the idea of empty land were perfectly aware that others lived there. What they denied was not presence but a particular kind of relationship to the land, one that their own law was prepared to recognise as ownership.

The intellectual resources for that denial were readily available. An influential strand of early modern European thought held that property arose when a person mixed his labour with the earth, enclosing it, ploughing it and improving it; John Locke's well-known version of the argument drew explicitly on the example of America. On this view, land that was hunted over, gathered from, burned to renew its grasses or moved across with the seasons had not yet been appropriated, however long people had lived on it. The settlers' error, if it was one, was not a failure to see but a theory of what was worth seeing. Fences, fields and permanent buildings registered as use; songs that mapped water sources, rules governing who might hunt where, and the careful management of fire did not.

This matters because the doctrine has now been formally repudiated in several places, most famously when the High Court of Australia in 1992 rejected the proposition that the continent had belonged to no one at the time of British settlement, a proposition on which much of the country's land law had quietly rested. The repudiation was a real achievement, and it would be ungracious to belittle it. Yet the regimes of native title that followed typically require claimants to show that they have maintained a continuing connection with the land under traditional laws and customs, often to the satisfaction of courts that weigh written records and documented occupation heavily. Communities that were driven off their country, or whose practices were suppressed, find that the very dispossession they complain of becomes evidence against them: a broken connection, however it was broken, is a weaker claim.

In other words, the factual premise of terra nullius has been abandoned while something like its theoretical premise survives. The burden has simply been reversed. Where the settler state once declared the land empty and left the inhabitants to prove otherwise in practice, the modern claimant must now prove, in a forum designed by the settler state, that the land was never emptied of them.

It does not follow that recognition is worthless, or that some purer form of restitution is readily available. Any legal system must have rules of proof, and claims that could be made without evidence would invite abuse. The point is narrower. A doctrine is not fully dismantled when its conclusion is reversed if the standard used to apply the reversal was shaped by the doctrine itself. To finish the work would require asking not only whether people were present, which no one now denies, but whose ways of being present count as having a place.

The unbundled view is less satisfying than either of the stories it replaces, since it yields no single date and no single villain. The first is commonly described as civic and open, since it admits anyone born on the land regardless of ancestry; the second as ethnic and closed, since it ties membership to bloodline. The contrast is useful as a first approximation, but as a guide to which societies are more welcoming it is close to misleading.

Consider why states adopted the soil rule in the first place. Where governments needed to populate large territories and to secure soldiers and taxpayers, it was convenient to claim the children of newcomers automatically, whatever their parents' wishes or origins. The rule expressed less a generous conception of belonging than an interest in numbers. Conversely, the descent rule has served countries with large populations abroad, allowing the children of emigrants to retain a connection to a homeland they may visit only occasionally. Neither motive is obviously more liberal than the other, and the same rule can serve quite different ends depending on who adopts it and why.

More importantly, the rule that governs birth is only one part of a citizenship regime, and often not the part that matters most. A state that follows the descent principle but allows long-term residents to naturalise after a few years, and extends citizenship to children who have grown up and been schooled in the country, may be far easier to join than a state that follows the soil principle but makes naturalisation slow, costly and uncertain for adults. The second state includes every child born on its territory and keeps their parents waiting at the door indefinitely. A single household may then contain citizens and foreigners under one roof, divided by nothing more than the place where each member happened to be born.

None of this means the choice of birth rule is unimportant. The soil principle does guarantee something valuable: no one born in the territory can be left, by the mere fact of birth, a permanent outsider in the only country he has known, and states without it have sometimes produced generations of residents with no secure status. But that guarantee is better understood as one protection among several than as the defining mark of an open society. The question worth asking of any nationality law is not which principle it starts from, but how readily it turns people who have made their lives in a country into its members.

What matters is not whether powers expire but how hard it is to extend them. Every entrenched constitution invites an awkward question. If a majority of citizens today wishes to change some arrangement, why should it be prevented from doing so by rules that were drafted generations ago by people who are now dead, and who could not have imagined the conditions under which their successors live? A requirement that amendments win two-thirds of the legislature, or the approval of most regions, looks from this angle like a device for letting the past outvote the present. The objection has a democratic edge that is hard to dismiss: it asks by what right one generation sets the terms on which another must govern itself. Nor is it answered by praising the wisdom of the framers, since wisdom about one century is no warrant for authority over the next.

The most familiar answer appeals to self-binding. Just as a sailor who knows he will be tempted by a dangerous song may order his crew to tie him to the mast and ignore his later pleas, a people may bind itself in a calm moment against the passions it expects to feel in an agitated one. Entrenchment, on this account, is not the past overruling the present but the present protecting itself from its own worst impulses. The image is attractive, but it does not fit the case. The sailor binds himself, and the man who later strains at the ropes is the same man who gave the order. A constitution binds people who were not present when it was adopted and who gave no order at all. To call this self-binding is to assume precisely the continuity of the collective self that the objection calls into question.

A better defence starts from a different picture of what entrenchment does. Amendment rules do not, in practice, pit the living against the dead, because the dead cast no votes. The protection is not merely theoretical: a group that knows it can block changes to its basic standing has less reason to fear the ordinary swings of electoral fortune, and more reason to accept defeat on everything else. The merchant in an oasis town did not need to know the distant market where the silk would end up; he needed to know the caravan leader who would buy it from him and the one who had sold it to him. Long-distance trade, on this view, was an accumulation of short-distance relationships.

This has consequences that the romantic image hides. Each story can be furnished with abundant evidence. What both tend to overlook is that the measures could work only with a degree of cooperation that neither officials nor force could guarantee.

Quarantine depends, at its most basic, on information. A household must be shut up before the disease spreads from it, which means that someone must report the first case, and the people best placed to report it are the members of the household themselves, together with their neighbours. Every one of them had reasons to stay silent. A shut household lost its income; its members might be removed to an isolation hospital from which many did not return; its bedding and clothing might be burned. Families that concealed a death, bribed an inspector or slipped out of the city before the doors were sealed were not simply defying authority. They were responding sensibly to the costs that reporting imposed on them, and neighbours who might have informed on them faced, in their turn, the prospect of being shut up beside them.

Where the measures had any success, it seems to have been because authorities, sometimes reluctantly, took on obligations in return. Such provisions were expensive and often inadequate, and the surviving records do not allow us to say with any confidence how much difference they made to mortality. Severity could secure compliance from those already known to the authorities; it could not make the unknown come forward.

Their success is limited by what disclosure costs the person who discloses, and an authority that wishes to know must first make knowing bearable.

The respectable stayed indoors; the streets belonged to watchmen, to those whose trades required darkness, to lovers, thieves and the young. This was not freedom in any simple sense, for the dark was also where violence went unpunished. It was, however, a separate jurisdiction, a time in which the authority of the day ran only weakly and in which much that could not be done openly was done.

The first systematic street lighting was installed less to lengthen the working day than to bring this jurisdiction under control.
