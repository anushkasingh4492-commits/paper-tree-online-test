Discussions of the unreliable narrator tend to treat it as a game of detection. A narrator tells us one thing, the novel quietly lets us see another, and the pleasure lies in catching the gap: the jealous husband who insists he is perfectly calm, the child who reports adult quarrels without understanding them. Critics have catalogued the signals by which such narrators give themselves away, the protest that is too insistent, the detail that contradicts a claim made a page earlier, and readers have learned to watch for them. On this account the device is a kind of puzzle, and a reader who has spotted the discrepancy has finished the work the book asked of her. The narrator was wrong; the reader, standing above him, is right.

This picture is not false, but it is thin. It assumes that behind every unreliable account there stands a reliable one, which the attentive reader reconstructs and then keeps. Yet the clues by which we detect a narrator's distortions are supplied by the same narrator. We infer that the husband is jealous from what he notices, what he repeats, what he cannot stop describing; we have no other witness. The true story we assemble is therefore itself an interpretation, built from the materials of the account we have decided to distrust, and we hold it with a confidence that the novel has done nothing to guarantee.

What the best uses of the device achieve, then, is not the replacement of a bad narrator by a good reader but a change in how the reader regards narration as such. Having learned to read one voice against itself, we begin to notice how much we ordinarily grant to voices that give no sign of strain: the calm third-person narrator of a realist novel, the memoirist, the friend recounting a quarrel. None of them is lying, perhaps, but all of them select, arrange and omit. The difference is one of degree rather than kind; the narrator we call reliable is simply one whose selections we have not thought to question. The unreliable narrator makes visible a condition that reliable narration merely conceals.

It must be admitted that not every novel built on an untrustworthy teller rises to this. Some use the device as a trapdoor, a revelation held back for the final chapters that turns everything before it into misdirection. Such books can be thrilling once. On a second reading, however, they tend to go flat, because the only question they posed has been answered. The novels that survive rereading are those in which the distrust never quite settles, in which the reader, having caught the narrator out, is left wondering what she herself has failed to see.

A persistent habit of literary talk treats the short story as a smaller novel: the same materials of character, setting and incident, simply supplied in lesser quantities. On this view the story is where writers practise before attempting the larger form, and a collection of stories is a kind of portfolio of novels not yet written. The habit is reinforced by publishers, who have long regarded collections as harder to sell, and by critics who praise a story for being 'as rich as a novel', as though richness of that kind were the measure of success. Prizes and anthologies have sometimes followed suit, treating a writer's stories as preparation and the first novel as the real debut.

The comparison misunderstands where a short story does its work. A novel accumulates; it earns its effects through duration, letting the reader live alongside its people for enough days that change in them can be felt as change. A story has no such time to spend. What it has instead is selection, and selection pressed hard enough becomes a kind of meaning in itself. When a story shows a woman packing a suitcase, pausing over a single photograph and putting it back in the drawer, the eleven years of marriage that the story never describes are present precisely because they are withheld. The reader does not receive them; she supplies them, and the supplying is the experience.

This is why expanding a fine story so rarely improves it. Give the woman a history, a sister to confide in, a chapter on the courtship, and the photograph becomes one detail among many rather than the point on which everything turns. The gaps that had been doing the work are filled with information, and information, however well written, is less charged than implication. The reader who had been supplying the marriage is now simply told about it, and being told is a lesser kind of participation. The story's brevity was not a limitation it laboured under but the condition of its force.

None of this means that stories and novels never meet. Some writers have plainly used stories as sketches, and more than one long novel began as a few pages its author could not leave alone. Nor is brevity a virtue in itself; a weak story can hide behind its shortness, gesturing at depths it has not earned. The claim is narrower: that when a story succeeds on its own terms, it succeeds by a method the novel mostly cannot use, and that judging it by the novel's standards will reliably mistake its strengths for omissions.

Among serious readers there is a settled suspicion of the happy ending. A novel that closes with a wedding, a reconciliation or a recovered fortune is assumed to have flinched at the last moment, to have given its audience what it wanted rather than what its story required. An ending in death, separation or failure, by contrast, is credited with courage. The assumption behind the contrast is rarely stated but easy to reconstruct: life does not resolve itself neatly, so a book that resolves neatly must be telling a comforting lie. The suspicion is old enough to have become a reflex, and few readers who share it could say where they acquired it.

There is something to this, since the history of popular fiction is crowded with endings that arrive from outside the story, the unexpected legacy, the villain who repents in a paragraph, the rival who conveniently dies, and readers are right to feel cheated by them. Such endings resemble a rescue by a passing stranger: welcome, perhaps, but unconnected with anything the characters have done. But the fault in such endings is not their happiness. It is that nothing in the preceding chapters made them necessary. A catastrophe imposed in the same way, a heroine killed by a runaway carriage because the author wanted the book to feel weighty, is no less arbitrary and, being arbitrary, no more truthful.

The prejudice persists, I think, because it confuses two things that endings do. Every ending closes; it marks the point after which the story will not be told. Some endings also console; they leave the reader reassured about the world the book has shown. Closure is a formal necessity, since a story must stop somewhere, while consolation is a choice. The suspicious reader notices that happy endings console, objects to the consolation, and then mistakes the objection for an argument against closure itself, as though the honest book would be the one that refused to finish. Yet an unhappy ending closes just as firmly; it simply closes on a different note, and that note can be just as comfortably familiar to readers who have learned to expect gloom from serious fiction.

The better test, then, is an internal one: an ending is honest when it is the place towards which the book's own pressures have been carrying it, so that a reader looking back can see how the earlier pages prepared it, whether that place is a marriage or a grave. By this test some happy endings are among the hardest-won things in fiction, and some tragic ones are merely fashionable. The question to ask of an ending is not whether it pleases but whether it follows.

The line between literary and genre fiction is usually drawn in terms of freedom. The literary novel, it is said, invents its own shape, while the crime novel, the romance and the tale of space travel arrive with their shapes already supplied: a body in the first chapter, an obstacle between the lovers, a voyage, a crisis and a return. To write in a genre is, on this account, to fill in a form someone else designed, and the best a genre writer can hope for is to fill it in with unusual skill. Critics who accept this account tend to praise a crime novel by saying that it rises above its genre, as though the genre were something to be escaped.

The account has the logic of constraint backwards. Nobody thinks a sonnet a lesser poem because its length and rhyme scheme were fixed before the poet began. We understand that the fixed form is what makes certain effects possible: a turn in the ninth line registers as a turn only because the reader knows where turns are expected. Genre conventions work in much the same way. A reader of detective stories knows that the culprit must be among the people introduced, that the clues must be fair, that a solution will come. These expectations are not a cage around the writer but an instrument she can play, and the slightest departure from them, a detective who is wrong, a solution that satisfies the law but not the reader, is felt with a precision no unconventional novel can command, because the unconventional novel has no settled expectation to depart from.

This is also why devoted readers of a genre are often its most exacting critics. Having read a hundred novels built on the same frame, they are alert to the smallest variation in it, much as a chess player who knows an opening thoroughly notices a single unusual move that a novice would pass over. Such readers can tell at once whether a twist is earned or merely borrowed, and they are rarely generous to a writer who mistakes one for the other. Their pleasure is not the passive pleasure of repetition but the active one of comparison.

None of this rescues every genre book. Conventions harden into formula when a writer treats them as a checklist to be completed rather than an expectation to be worked against, and a great deal of genre fiction is written in exactly this way. But the same is true of literary fiction, which has its own conventions, the quiet revelation in the final paragraph, the fractured chronology, the unnamed narrator, and its own writers who complete them dutifully. The useful distinction is not between books that have conventions and books that lack them, since none lack them, but between books that do something with their conventions and books that merely obey them.

The commonest objection to free verse is that it is merely prose with the margins moved. Take away the rhyme and the regular metre, the objection runs, and nothing distinguishes a poem from a paragraph except the typographer's decision to stop each line short of the edge of the page. If that decision is arbitrary, the poem is a pretence; if it is not, the objector would like to know what principle governs it, and a vague appeal to the poet's ear or instinct will not satisfy him.

The question deserves a better answer than poets usually give it, and the principle, where there is one, is that the line is a unit of attention. A reader pauses, however slightly, at the end of a line, and in that pause whatever the line has said is briefly complete. A sentence broken across two lines is therefore read twice: once as the line presents it and once as the sentence finally does. Consider a line that ends 'she said she would not leave', followed by one that begins 'the house until the rain had stopped'. For the length of the pause the woman refuses to leave at all; the next line shrinks the refusal to a matter of weather. Neither reading cancels the other; the poem holds the larger refusal and the smaller one together, and prose, which delivers its sentences whole, cannot easily do this.

Line breaks also govern pace, since a poem made of short lines slows the reader, weighting each word with a pause, while long lines carry her forward faster than the sense alone would. Where metre once supplied a regular beat against which a poet could set the natural stresses of speech, the free verse poet works with the silence at the ends of lines, which is a cruder instrument than metre but not a negligible one, and which has the compensating advantage that it can be varied from line to line without breaking any rule.

None of this shows that the objection is always wrong. A good deal of what is published as free verse is broken into lines for no reason a reader could recover, and in such cases the objection is simply correct: the lines could be run together without loss. A fair test is exactly that: set the poem out as prose, read it again, and ask what has gone. If nothing is lost, if no meaning that the breaks created has vanished and no pace has been flattened, then the breaks were decoration. What the test cannot do is condemn the form as such, since in the poems that pass it the breaks turn out to have been doing work that the prose version cannot preserve.

Few critical preferences are as widely shared as the preference for round characters over flat ones. A round character, in the usual formulation, has an inner life complex enough that it can change, contradict itself and still remain recognisable; a flat character is built around a single trait and can be summed up in a sentence. The first is praised as lifelike, the second tolerated as a convenience, useful for filling a drawing room or delivering a message but not to be mistaken for the real achievement of fiction.

Yet the preference rests on an assumption that deserves scrutiny: that the novel's central business is the representation of individual inner life. That has been the business of one powerful tradition, but it is not the only thing novels do. Many of the novels readers return to are as much about a society as about a self, and a society is felt in fiction partly through the people who never change: the aunt who has said the same thing at every family dinner for thirty years, the official for whom every request is a breach of procedure. Their fixity is not a failure of imagination. It is how the novel registers the pressure of a world that does not bend, against which its few changing figures can be measured.

Flat characters also carry much of fiction's comedy, which depends on repetition. A man who responds to every misfortune by consulting the same almanac is funny the third time precisely because he could not have done otherwise. Deepen him, give him a moment of doubt about the almanac, and the joke dissolves into pathos; this may be a gain, but it is a different book. There is, too, a case that flatness is closer to ordinary experience than critics allow. Most of the people we know, we know only by a few habits and phrases, and a novel that rendered every acquaintance in the round would misrepresent how other people actually appear to us, however much it flattered our sense of their depth.

This is not to invert the hierarchy. A novel peopled entirely by flat characters would be a cartoon, and the moment when a figure we had thought simple reveals unexpected depth remains one of fiction's great effects, though it depends on our having first taken him as simple. The point is that flat and round are not grades of achievement but different tools, suited to different tasks, and a criticism that rewards only one of them will misread every novel in which both are at work.

The word from which the essay takes its name once meant an attempt or a trial, the act of testing something by weighing it, and the form has never quite outgrown that sense. An essay, in its original spirit, is not the report of a conclusion already reached but the record of a mind trying a thought to see whether it will bear weight. It may change direction halfway through, concede more than it intended, or end with a question sharper than the one it started with. These are not lapses. They are the form doing what it was invented to do, and the reader of such an essay is permitted to overhear a mind in conversation with itself rather than merely to receive the outcome of that conversation.

It is worth noticing how far the essay most people now encounter has moved from this. The essay that is taught in schools and demanded in universities opens with a thesis, marshals evidence in its favour, dispatches objections and restates the thesis at the close. It is, in effect, a miniature treatise, and its virtues are those of the treatise: clarity of claim, economy of proof, a structure the reader can grasp in advance. These are real virtues, and there are good reasons to teach them, not least that a writer who cannot state a claim plainly is in no position to test it. But the result is that a generation of writers has learned to regard any movement of thought within a piece of writing as a defect of planning, something to be removed in revision so that the finished text looks as though its conclusion had been visible from the first sentence.

What is lost is not merely a style. The provisional essay offers something the treatise structurally cannot: a view of how a conclusion is reached, including the paths that were tried and abandoned. A reader who watches a writer test an idea against an example, find that it fails, and adjust it, learns not only where the writer arrived but how much the arrival cost and what it excluded. She is reminded, too, that the conclusion was reached by someone who could have been wrong and who knows it. The treatise presents its conclusion as if it were the only place the evidence could lead; the essay, by showing the alternatives it passed through, leaves the reader better placed to disagree. In this sense its apparent modesty is a form of candour about the limits of its own claims.

The same candour explains the essayist's much criticised first person. The 'I' of the essay is often taken for self-display, and sometimes it is exactly that. More often it marks the scope of a claim: this is how the matter looks from here, to someone with this experience, and the reader is invited to test it against her own. The impersonal voice of the treatise, by contrast, claims a standpoint from nowhere in particular, and so makes its claims harder to locate and therefore harder to contest. Its objectivity is sometimes real, but it is always also a manner, and a manner can be adopted by writers whose claims have not earned it.

None of this licenses formlessness. Provisionality is easily counterfeited, and the meandering essay that circles its subject without ever risking a claim is as familiar as the rigid one, and less useful. A thought cannot be tested unless it is first stated with enough definiteness to fail. A genuine attempt is one that might not succeed, and an essay that cannot fail because it never claims anything has not attempted anything at all. The essay at its best is therefore not the opposite of argument but argument conducted in the open, where the reader can see each step taken and judge for herself whether it holds.

Modern criticism has tended to treat this fact as an extenuating circumstance. When such a novel sprawls, introduces a character it later forgets, or closes a chapter on a contrived moment of suspense, the serial is invoked as an excuse: the author was writing to deadline, paid by length, obliged to bring readers back next month. The implication is that the real novel, the one the author would have written in better conditions, lies somewhere beneath the one we have, and that serial publication is a kind of noise to be filtered out.

The trouble with this view is that it treats the serial only as a set of pressures, when it was also a set of possibilities, and many of the qualities we now admire in these books grew from it. A novel read over eighteen months is lived with in a way that a novel read over a week is not. Its characters age alongside their readers; its crises arrive amid the readers' own. The large casts and interwoven plots that later critics found unwieldy were in part a response to this rhythm, since a reader returning after a month's absence needed several lines of interest to pick up, and recurring figures with distinctive habits of speech helped her find her place. What looks in a single volume like digression could, at the pace of first reading, serve as the texture of a world one returned to.

Serial publication also made the novel unusually public while it was still unfinished. An instalment could be discussed before the next was written, and authors could, and sometimes did, adjust their plans in light of how readers responded. A purist may regard this as a surrender of artistic control. It can as easily be seen as a kind of conversation, in which a book took shape partly through its encounter with an audience rather than being delivered complete to readers whose only role was to receive it. For a time the novel became a shared event, followed by readers who knew that a great many others were reading the same pages in the same weeks.

None of this is to deny the costs. Writing to fixed lengths did produce padding, and the demand for suspense at the end of each part did produce cliffhangers that the story did not need and that read awkwardly once the parts were bound together. A reader of the collected volume is entitled to notice these. That ideal has a history of its own, and it gained much of its authority later, among writers and critics who worked for a different market and under different conditions. Applied backward, it does not so much reveal the faults of serial fiction as convert its differences into faults.

There is, finally, an irony in the critical embarrassment. Every language lacks single words for countless things its speakers understand perfectly well.

This is not to say that nothing is lost when such a word is carried across. A single word is easy to reach for; a phrase is not. A word that ordinary speakers use daily carries the weight of all those uses, along with a register, a sound and a set of associations that no paraphrase reproduces. But the losses are of degree, not of kind, and they are not peculiar to exotic words. They occur whenever any word is rendered by another, and even within a single language, where no two synonyms carry quite the same associations. Seen this way, the romance of the untranslatable flatters the reader, who feels worldly for knowing the rare word, and it encourages a picture of cultures as sealed rooms whose inhabitants can only be gazed at, never understood. Arguments about the literary canon are usually conducted as arguments about a list. One side defends a set of books as the best that a tradition has produced; the other replies that the list was drawn up by a narrow group of readers and reflects their tastes and their blind spots. Both sides, for all their hostility, agree on what a canon is: a ranking, a roll of honour, a verdict on quality that can be revised by adding names or striking them out. It is worth asking whether that shared assumption is right.

A different account fits the actual history of canons better. On this account a work becomes canonical not because readers judge it excellent but because later writers cannot leave it alone. They answer it, parody it, borrow its situations, quarrel with its conclusions and rewrite it from the point of view of a character it neglected. The canonical work is then the one you must have read in order to understand a great many others; its status is less a medal than a position in a network of replies. This explains something the list model cannot. Canons contain works that almost everyone agrees are clumsy, uneven or dull in long stretches, and they exclude works of great finish that simply provoked no answer. A ranking by quality would not look like that; a record of a conversation would.

The account has a consequence for those who wish to change the canon. If canonical status is conferred by later writing rather than by verdict, then a syllabus committee cannot make a book canonical by placing it on a reading list, any more than a critic could remove one by declaring it overrated. What can be done is to put neglected works where future writers will meet them, in print, in translation and in the classroom, and then to wait and see whether they are taken up.

Yet the conversational canon is not thereby cleared of the exclusions its critics describe. Who gets to join a conversation depends on who was taught to read, who could find a publisher and whose replies were preserved. A tradition in which most writers came from one class or one sex will have conducted its conversation largely among them, and the canon it produced will reflect that. The conversational account does not answer the critics of the canon; it relocates their complaint, from the judgements of tastemakers to the conditions under which people could write at all, and that is where the remedy, if there is one, must be sought.

Translators of the great books of the past often remark on a puzzle. The original of an old epic or a classical tragedy does not seem to age; readers return to it century after century and find it as alive as ever. Yet translations of the same work date quickly. A version that seemed fresh to its first readers can sound stiff or quaint within a lifetime, and every generation or two a new one appears, prefaced by the claim that its predecessors have become unreadable. Why should the copy age when the original, which is older and in many ways stranger, does not? The question is worth more attention than it usually receives, because the answer says something about what a translation is.

The usual answer is linguistic: a translation is written in the language of its own moment, with that moment's idioms and its notions of what sounds dignified and what sounds plain, and these change. The original, by contrast, has been absorbed into a tradition that reads it as a historical text and forgives its strangeness. There is truth in this, but it cannot be the whole story, since old translations are often perfectly intelligible and still feel inadequate.

A deeper reason lies in what translation requires. A great work is full of places where its sense is undecided: a word that could mean anger or grief, a line whose tone could be mocking or tender, a scene whose moral weight is left to the reader. The original can leave such places open, and much of its power to sustain rereading comes from that openness; a translator has no such freedom, because writing in another language forces choices at every point, and each choice settles a question the original left unsettled. The choices are not arbitrary, but they are shaped by what the translator's own age finds plausible or important, and so the translation records one generation's reading of the work. When the concerns of that generation fade, its translation comes to look like what it always was: an interpretation.

Seen in this light, retranslation is not a sign that earlier translators failed. It is one of the ways a classic stays alive, each version making a fresh set of decisions and so showing a new reader something the others concealed. There is even an occasional reversal. Some translations become works of literature in their own right, read not as windows onto the original but for their own sake, and these, curiously, stop ageing in the way translations usually do; they come to be read, like originals, as historical texts. Their choices no longer look like a period's reading of another book; they look like the permanent features of a book of their own.

Few questions in criticism have been argued so long, or with so little sign of settlement, as whether the meaning of a work is what its author intended. The intentionalist says yes: a text is an utterance, utterances are made by someone for a purpose, and to ignore that purpose is to read not the work but a sequence of marks that happens to resemble it. The anti-intentionalist says no: the author's state of mind is usually unavailable, frequently confused and in any case beside the point, since a published work belongs to the public language in which it was written, and its meaning is whatever that language, competently read, makes of it.

Each side wins its arguments by making the other's view look absurd, and each is right about what it denies. The intentionalist is right that we cannot read without attributing purposes. To notice that a sentence is ironic, that a repetition is deliberate or that a sudden plainness of style marks a turning point is already to treat the text as the product of choices; strip away every assumption about purpose and the irony vanishes along with the design. The anti-intentionalist is right that a letter to a newspaper or a diary entry in which the author explains what the book means has no special authority over the book. Authors misremember, revise their motives after the fact and sometimes simply misjudge what they have made.

The mistake common to both is to picture intention as a private event lying behind the text, which the text either reports faithfully or conceals. Intention in writing is better understood the way we understand it in action. We do not usually discover what someone meant by watching them and then consulting their inner life; we read the purpose in the shape of what they did. In the same way the intentions that matter to criticism are those embodied in a work's choices, its emphases and omissions, the expectations it sets up and then disappoints. They are public because the work is.

This view does not make the author irrelevant, nor does it make the author final. A writer's notebooks may help us see a pattern we had missed, as the testimony of a witness may help a court, but the evidence is then the pattern, not the testimony. And a work may carry implications its author never foresaw and would perhaps have disowned: a novel written to celebrate a way of life can, through the very details it lovingly records, reveal the costs that way of life imposed on those at its margins. To point this out is not to defy the author's intention but to take the work's design more seriously than its author's later account of it.

Schools encourage the same view without meaning to, since a syllabus moves on once a book has been 'done', as though a single passage through it were the whole of the work. The lament has real feeling behind it, and anyone who remembers staying up late to finish a novel knows what it describes.

There is a case for the opposite view, that in an important sense only rereading is reading. A first reading is dominated by one question, what happens next, and that question consumes most of our attention. We skim descriptions to reach events, miss the significance of details whose importance has not yet been revealed and form judgements of characters that the book later overturns. Only when we know the ending can we attend to how the book reaches it: the early scene that quietly prepares a later one, the image that returns with altered meaning, the minor character whose remark, seemingly idle at first, turns out to state the novel's theme. Knowing the outcome does not empty these details; it fills them. Consider a novel built around a concealed secret. Read a second time, its hints, which at first seemed part of the scenery, stand out as hints, and the reader can admire the skill with which they were hidden in plain sight, a pleasure that the first reader, hurrying towards the revelation, could not have had.

Rereading also reveals a change in the reader. The novel read at fifteen and again at forty has not altered a word, yet it is a different book, because the person reading it has acquired experiences that let different passages speak. A story of a marriage that seemed, to the young reader, a tale of dull people becomes, to the older one, almost unbearably exact. Nothing on the page has moved; what has moved is the range of experience the reader can bring to it, and with it the passages that seem to matter. In this sense a book reread is a kind of instrument for measuring oneself, and the measurements are often humbling: we discover what we were once unable to see, and sometimes that the passages we admired most were the weakest.

Some books, indeed, are exhausted by one passage through them, and this is perhaps the most useful test of all. The difference between the two kinds is not visible from inside a first reading, which is one more reason to distrust the verdicts we reach there.

Literary history has always been written from a remarkably small sample. Of the many thousands of novels published in a given century, a scholar can read a few hundred in a working life, and the histories of the form are built on a smaller set still, the books that later readers chose to keep in print. When such a history says that 'the novel' turned inward at a certain period, or abandoned the omniscient narrator, or grew preoccupied with the city, it is generalising from a selection that was never meant to be representative. Nobody chose the canon as a sample; it was assembled by generations of readers for reasons of taste, teaching and accident, and there is no reason to expect it to resemble the whole from which it was drawn. The advocates of computational or 'distant' reading start from this embarrassment. If machines can process an entire century's output, counting words, tracking the length of sentences or the frequency with which characters are named, then claims about the form as a whole can at last be tested against the whole.

The objections are familiar, and some are serious. Counting is not reading. The meaning of a word depends on its context, on irony, on the dramatic situation in which it is spoken, none of which a frequency table records. Critics add that many of the patterns such studies report are either trivial or confirm what attentive readers already knew, so that a great deal of machinery produces a modest result dressed in charts. There is also a subtler worry: the features a computer can count are those that can be defined mechanically, and these are not obviously the features that matter. A discipline that adopts such tools risks drifting towards studying what can be measured simply because it can be measured. Defenders of the method reply that every discipline begins by measuring crude things, but the reply concedes more than it denies.

Yet much of this debate rests on a false picture of the choice. Distant reading does not replace close reading so much as change what close reading is asked to do. A measure that shows some habit of style rising steadily for decades and then collapsing within a few years explains nothing by itself, but it tells a scholar exactly where to look. Such a finding means little until someone goes back and reads the books in question, and it is that return which produces understanding. The count poses a question; it does not answer one.

Where the method does make an independent contribution is in the matter of the sample. Even if a computer can say little about what any single novel means, it can show that a generalisation drawn from the canon fails to hold for the much larger body of books that the canon left out. That is not a trivial service. A claim about what 'the novel' did, which turns out to describe only the few hundred books that survived into the syllabus, was never a claim about the novel at all, but about the tastes of the readers who did the selecting. The modest promise of distant reading, then, is less to tell us what literature means than to tell us more accurately which literature our claims are about.

A familiar way of thinking about Western art music divides the creative labour with reassuring neatness. The composer makes the work and records it in a score; the performer's task is to realise the score faithfully, adding as little of himself as the music allows. On this view the score is the work, or at least its complete specification, and a performance is good to the degree that it delivers what the notation contains. Its attractions are considerable. It accounts for our confidence that two strikingly different performances are nevertheless performances of the same composition, and it furnishes the critic with a standard: the performer who alters the notes has made a mistake, whatever the result sounds like.

The difficulty is that notation specifies far less than the view requires. A score fixes pitches and their order with considerable precision, and durations rather less, but a great deal of what listeners hear as the character of a performance is left open or indicated only roughly. A marking that asks for a loud passage does not say how loud, and the right loudness in a small room is not the right loudness in a large hall. A word indicating a lively tempo admits a range of speeds wide enough to make one performance sound urgent and another relaxed. Nothing in the marks tells the player exactly how long to linger at the high point of a phrase, or how to weigh the notes of a chord against one another. These are not trivial details that a faithful performer can safely settle at random; they are much of what makes the music sound the way it does.

One response is to swing to the opposite extreme and say that the work simply is the performance, the score being no more than a set of instructions that each performer completes as she pleases. This rescues the performer's creative contribution, but at a considerable theoretical price. It cannot explain why some performances strike informed listeners as wrong-headed even when every note is in place, or why a performer who takes a slow movement at double speed has done something worse than make an unusual choice. If the score were merely a recipe to be finished at will, there would be no ground for such judgements, and yet musicians make them constantly and with considerable agreement.

That agreement points to what both positions conspicuously omit. Scores are never interpreted in isolation. They are read by musicians trained in a practice: a shared and largely unwritten understanding of what the marks mean in a given style, which conventions apply, what kinds of freedom are expected and where those freedoms stop. In music of one period a trill is understood to begin on the note above; in another it begins on the main note; the score is usually silent on the point, and the practice supplies the answer. The work, on this account, is neither the marks alone nor whatever a given performance makes of them, but the marks as a practice reads them. Fidelity is owed to that combination.

It may be objected that practices evolve, so that the work would apparently evolve with them, which seems absurd. But the objection establishes considerably less than it appears to. Practices do change, slowly, and the identity of a work is correspondingly less fixed than the first view supposed; that is a cost worth accepting, since it matches how musicians actually argue about performances. What does not follow is that anything goes. Few experiences in the visual world are as immediate or as hard to resist as the one produced by a large-eyed, round-faced, short-limbed creature stumbling towards us; we feel it before we have decided anything, and it is remarkably similar across cultures that agree on little else in matters of taste.

Students of animal behaviour noticed long ago that the features we find cute, a large head relative to the body, big low-set eyes, softness and clumsiness, are the features of the very young, and they proposed that the response is a mechanism for securing care. The explanation is plausible, but it accounts for less than it seems to. It tells us why infants elicit tenderness; it does not tell us why we find the same tenderness stirred by a cartoon kettle with eyes, or why the response is so readily extended to objects that need no care whatever. Once detached from the infant, the cute becomes a style, and a style can be applied to anything.

What makes the style interesting is its ambivalence. The cute object presents itself as small, soft and unable to resist, and our response to it is mixed in a way that tenderness towards a real child usually is not. Alongside the wish to protect, there is often a wish to possess and to handle, the half-joking urge to squeeze or pinch what one finds adorable, which people frequently admit to with some embarrassment. The cute flatters our power as much as it summons our care: it is an image of something that will never answer back.

This is why cuteness should be taken seriously rather than either indulged or sneered at. A quality that disarms judgement so quickly is a useful instrument for anyone who wishes to make something appear harmless, whether a product, an institution or a mascot standing in for an organisation whose activities are anything but soft. None of this means that delight in a kitten is a moral failing, and the pleasure is, in most of its forms, innocent enough. But an aesthetic that works by making us feel strong and kindly at once, and that does so before thought has begun, is not trivial; its very lightness is what gives it weight.

The maxim that there is no disputing about taste is usually offered as a courtesy. When two people disagree about a film or a piece of music, one of them, sensing that the conversation is about to turn sour, observes that it is all a matter of taste, and the other, relieved, agrees. The maxim seems to express a modern and admirable humility, for who is anyone to tell another person what to enjoy? But the courtesy rests on a confusion, and the confusion has consequences for the way we talk about art and, in the end, for the way we treat one another.

The confusion is between preference and judgement. If you dislike the taste of coriander and I like it, there is nothing for us to argue about. Neither of us thinks the other has missed something; we simply report different reactions, and the reports do not compete, any more than your being cold and my being warm in the same room amounts to a disagreement about the room. Disagreements about novels or buildings are rarely like this. When I say that a novel is sentimental and you say that it is moving, each of us means to be saying something about the book, and each of us expects the other to be able, at least in principle, to recognise it. We point to passages, compare the novel with others, and draw attention to what the other may have overlooked. These are the moves of people who believe there is something to be right about, even if neither of them can prove it.

The relativist replies that this behaviour, however common, rests on an illusion. There is no fact about the book's sentimentality independent of readers' reactions, the argument runs, and so our disputes, unlike disputes about the boiling point of water, can never be settled; at most one party tires and gives up. This reply assumes that a dispute is worth having only if it can end in proof. Aesthetic argument, however, does not aim at proof. It aims at a change in perception, and its success is the moment when you look again at the passage I have pointed to and hear the false note I heard, or when I, rereading it at your insistence, find that what I took for manipulation is in fact restraint. Such moments are not rare. Most people can remember a friend, a teacher or a critic who showed them something they had not been able to see, and they do not remember the experience as having been talked into a preference; they remember it as having learned something.

It must be granted that some disagreements reach a floor. Two careful readers may agree about every feature of a poem, its rhythm, its imagery, its debts and its departures, and still differ about whether it is good, because one prizes austerity and the other abundance. At this point argument may genuinely run out, and it is sensible to say so. But the floor lies far lower than the maxim implies. Most disputes about taste break off long before they reach it, not because the parties have exhausted their reasons, but because one of them has invoked the maxim in order to end the conversation.

There is, finally, something less humble in the maxim than it appears. To tell someone that their view of a novel is merely a matter of taste is to decline to treat it as a view at all, that is, as a claim that might be true and from which one might learn. The maxim protects both parties from the risk of being wrong, but it does so at the price of the possibility of being changed. Real respect for another person's judgement would consist in disputing it.

When readers began, in large numbers, to write and circulate their own stories about the characters of popular novels and television series, the reaction from some authors was indignation. The characters were theirs, they said; to send them on adventures their creators had never imagined, to pair them with new lovers or to bring the dead back to life was a kind of trespass. Defenders of the practice replied with equal heat that a story, once released into the world, belongs to those who love it, and that the fans who keep a fictional world alive with their own inventions have a better claim to it than the person who merely began it. Both sides, I think, have misdescribed what is at stake.

Consider first how unusual the authors' position is, historically speaking. For most of the history of storytelling, retelling was the ordinary way in which stories lived. The same heroes passed from one teller to another, gaining episodes, companions and endings along the way; a poet who took up an old legend was expected to change it, and was admired for the changes. The idea that a fictional person belongs to a single creator, and that others may not set that person in new situations, took shape alongside printing, markets for books and laws that made authorship a form of property. It is not a natural fact about stories, though it now feels like one. Fan writers, from this angle, are less the vandals of a settled order than the late heirs of a much older one.

Yet the fans' champions claim too much when they turn this history into a counter-theory of ownership. To say that a story belongs to those who love it is still to speak of belonging, merely moving the title deeds from one party to another. What the older practice suggests is rather that stories are not the sort of thing that is owned in the first place, though particular tellings are. An author can reasonably object to having her text copied and sold under someone else's name, or to having her own work altered and passed off as hers. It is much harder to see what she loses when a reader imagines her characters on another afternoon, so long as no one mistakes that afternoon for one she wrote. The distinction between a text and a world is precisely the one that both sides have blurred.

There is an irony here that deserves notice. Fan communities, which began by contesting an author's exclusive authority, have often developed authorities of their own. Long debates arise over what counts as 'canon', over which inventions are faithful and which are betrayals, and newcomers who ignore the accumulated rules can be treated with a severity that few authors would dare to show their readers. In such moments the community reproduces, on a smaller scale, the very proprietary attitude it set out to resist, as though the impulse to fix a story and guard its boundaries were not peculiar to authors but a temptation that comes with loving any story at all.

None of this settles every case, and there are surely retellings that wound in ways the distinction does not capture, as when a living author's characters are put to uses she finds degrading. But it does suggest a more useful question than the one with which the quarrel began, which asked whose story it is. The better question is what each kind of claim protects: the integrity of a particular telling, which deserves defence, or the boundaries of an imagined world, which, on the evidence of the long history of storytelling, have never been defensible for long.
