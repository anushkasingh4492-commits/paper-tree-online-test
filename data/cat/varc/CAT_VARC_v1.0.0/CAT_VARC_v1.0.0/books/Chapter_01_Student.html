A familiar picture of the responsible thinker holds that she believes nothing on another's say-so until she has satisfied herself that the speaker is reliable. On this view, testimony is a second-class source of knowledge, legitimate only when it can be cashed out in the currency of one's own perception and reasoning: I may believe the timetable because I have found timetables accurate before, and I may believe my doctor because I have, or could obtain, evidence of her competence. The picture has the appeal of self-reliance. It seems to keep the knower in charge of what she knows.

The difficulty is that almost nobody could meet its demands, and the few who tried would know very little. Consider how much of what an ordinary adult believes was simply told to her: the shape of the continents, the date of her own birth, the existence of cells, the fact that a city she has never visited lies where the maps place it. To check the reliability of these sources she would have to rely on further sources, whose reliability would in turn need checking. The independent track record the picture asks for is itself largely assembled from testimony. A child who refused to believe her teachers until she had verified their credentials would never acquire the language in which credentials are described.

It does not follow that we may believe whatever we are told. The better view is that a hearer is entitled to accept what she is told unless she has some reason for doubt: a speaker with an evident motive to deceive, a claim that collides with what she already has good grounds to believe, a manner that suggests guesswork rather than knowledge. On this view the burden is reversed. Trust is the default, and it is suspicion, not acceptance, that must be earned by evidence. This is not credulity, because the default is sensitive to defeaters; the hearer who ignores a glaring sign of deceit is not exercising her entitlement but neglecting it.

Defenders of the stricter picture are right about one thing. A default of trust is only as good as the hearer's alertness to the signals that should override it, and that alertness is unevenly distributed and easily exploited. The con artist's whole craft consists in suppressing the cues that would trigger doubt. But this shows that we need to cultivate sensitivity to defeaters, not that we must abandon trust as a starting point. A community in which everyone demanded proof before belief would not be more rational than ours; it would merely be poorer in what it could collectively know.

The sceptic's argument has an irritating simplicity. You claim to know that you are sitting in a room reading. But if you were dreaming vividly, or if your experiences were being produced by some elaborate deception, everything would seem exactly as it now does. Since you cannot tell from the inside that you are not in such a situation, you cannot rule it out; and if you cannot rule it out, the sceptic concludes, you do not know that you are reading. The argument generalises to almost everything, and many careful attempts to refute it have the air of a lawyer winning on a technicality rather than of anyone actually seeing the truth.

One response is to concede the argument and shrug: perhaps we know nothing, but we must act as if we did, and so nothing of practical importance changes. This concession is too generous. It treats the sceptic's standard, that knowing requires excluding every alternative one cannot distinguish from the actual situation, as if it were simply the correct standard, which we happen to fall short of. Yet we nowhere apply that standard outside the philosophy seminar. A birdwatcher who reports a heron on the riverbank is not asked whether she has excluded the possibility of a mechanical decoy indistinguishable at that distance; she is asked whether she looked carefully, knows her birds and had a clear view. If a park ranger has just announced that decoys have been placed along that stretch of river, the question changes, and it is right that it should.

What this suggests is that the alternatives a knower must exclude are fixed by something other than bare conceivability: by what is live in the circumstances, by what a reasonable person has some ground to take seriously. The sceptic does not discover a hidden flaw in our ordinary claims; he changes the subject by importing a standard that no ordinary claim was ever meant to satisfy. This is why his argument feels at once unanswerable and beside the point.

None of this makes scepticism worthless. Its exaggerated standard has the merit of exposing how much of our confidence rests on background assumptions we rarely state, and in particular circumstances, the decoy case among them, those assumptions deserve scrutiny. The error lies in moving from the observation that every assumption could in principle be questioned to the conclusion that every assumption must be questioned before anything is known. The first is a useful reminder; the second would make knowledge impossible by definition, and a definition that guarantees its own emptiness is a poor account of what we were trying to describe.

When two experts disagree about a question the rest of us cannot assess, it can seem that the layperson has only two options: to become an expert herself, or to pick a side at random. Neither is attractive. The first is usually impossible within the time available, and the second makes a mockery of the idea that belief should answer to evidence. But the dilemma is false. Although the layperson cannot judge the dispute directly, she can often judge the disputants, and judgements of that kind can be reasonable even when the subject matter remains opaque to her.

Several kinds of evidence are available. She can ask whether either expert has a record of predictions that can be checked, and how those predictions fared. She can ask whether one of them stands to gain from being believed, through money, reputation or loyalty to an institution. She can ask how the rest of the field divides, bearing in mind that a majority counts for more when its members reached their views independently than when they all learned them from the same teacher. None of these questions requires understanding the dispute itself. Each concerns the circumstances in which a claim is advanced rather than its technical content, and each can be answered, at least roughly, by an attentive citizen with no specialised training whatever.

One tempting source of evidence is less useful than it looks. Laypeople often favour the expert who argues more fluently, answers objections more quickly and seems more at ease. Yet these are skills of performance, and they are only loosely connected with being right. A careful scientist may hedge, pause and admit uncertainties that a confident charlatan would brush aside, and an audience that rewards confidence is, in effect, rewarding the very habit that should make it suspicious. The difficulty is compounded by the fact that public debate, with its brief exchanges and its appetite for memorable phrases, is arranged almost entirely so as to display performance, and so to favour the disputant who has mastered it.

It would be a mistake to conclude that the layperson's position is comfortable. Track records are often unavailable for the questions that matter most, interests can be hidden, and a field can be captured by a fashion that makes its consensus less independent than it appears. The indirect methods narrow the range of reasonable belief; they rarely close it. Still, a choice made on such grounds is not a coin toss, and treating it as one hands an advantage to whoever is most willing to exploit the public's sense of helplessness.

Suppose you and a colleague whom you regard as your equal in competence and care examine the same evidence and reach opposite conclusions. Neither of you has information the other lacks; neither has reason to think the other was tired or distracted. What should you do? An influential answer holds that you should move towards your colleague's view, giving her opinion the same weight as your own. The thought behind it is simple. From a neutral standpoint, the disagreement is evidence that one of you has erred, and you have no reason, independent of the dispute itself, to think the error is hers rather than yours. To stand firm would be to treat the mere fact that a conclusion is yours as a reason to favour it.

This principle seems to me correct over a wide range of cases, and it is more demanding than most people find comfortable. It condemns the familiar move of discounting a dissenter precisely because she dissents: 'she is usually so sensible, but on this question she has plainly lost her way'. If the only evidence that she has lost her way is that she disagrees with you, the reasoning simply assumes what is in question.

Yet the principle cannot be applied without qualification. Imagine two equally able auditors reviewing the same short ledger, one of whom concludes that the accounts balance while the other reports that the firm's assets exceed the number of grains of sand on the earth. The first auditor need not split the difference. Her confidence that her colleague has blundered does not rest on her own conclusion about the ledger; it rests on the absurdity of his, which she can recognise without relying on the reasoning they disagree about. What licenses holding firm, then, is not that a view is one's own but that one has grounds, independent of the dispute, for locating the error on the other side. The equal-weight principle survives, but it turns out to be a claim about what to do when such independent grounds are absent, not a rule that applies whatever one knows.

Critics sometimes press a further objection: since thoughtful people disagree about the equal-weight principle itself, the principle seems to require its own defenders to lose confidence in it. This is less damaging than it first appears. A principle about how to respond to disagreement may well recommend reduced confidence in itself without thereby becoming false; it would show only that the matter is genuinely hard, which no one denied. What the objection does reveal is that conciliation cannot be the whole story about rational belief. A thinker who revised every view the moment a peer dissented would soon have no settled views left to revise, and some account is still owed of what entitles anyone to a starting point.

We tend to assume that each of us has a special and nearly infallible access to our own minds. Others must infer what I think and feel from my speech and behaviour, whereas I, it seems, am acquainted with my own mental states directly, without any need for inference or interpretation. For some states the assumption is plausible. If I sincerely report a sharp pain in my knee, it is hard to see what better evidence anyone could have, including me, about whether the pain is there. But the assumption is usually extended, without much reflection, to a quite different class of facts: the facts about why I did what I did.

Here the case for special access is far weaker. Imagine a shopper who chooses one of several identical scarves laid out in a row and who, asked why, cites the richer colour or the finer weave of the one she took. Since the scarves really are identical, her explanation cannot be true, yet it is offered sincerely and without hesitation. What she supplies is not a report of a cause she observed operating within her but a plausible reconstruction, drawn from a shared and largely unexamined theory of human motivation, of the kind anyone might give of why a person would choose a scarf. An onlooker armed with the same commonsense theory could have produced the same explanation, and would have been no more and no less likely to be right.

It is tempting to conclude from such cases that self-knowledge is an illusion and that we stand to ourselves essentially as strangers do, but that conclusion overreaches. The shopper's error concerns the causes of a past choice, and about such causes she may well be no better placed than anyone else. But there is another kind of question about which her position is not that of an observer at all. If she is asked which scarf she will wear tomorrow, or whether she thinks scarves make a sensible gift, she does not answer by studying evidence about herself; she answers by deciding, or by considering the merits of scarves. Her authority here derives from the fact that the answer is, in part, up to her.

The lesson, then, is not that the first person has no privilege but that its privilege has been misplaced. We are poorly placed to discover, by looking inward, the forces that moved us; we are well placed to settle what we will stand by. Confusing the two invites a double error: an unearned confidence about our own motives, and an exaggerated scepticism that would deny us even the authority we exercise whenever we make up our minds.

'I did not know' is among the oldest excuses there is, and often among the best. We do not blame a pharmacist who dispenses a medicine that was contaminated at the factory in a way no inspection could detect, nor a traveller who carries a parcel for a friend without any reason to suspect what it contains. In such cases ignorance breaks the link between what a person did and what she intended, and blame that disregarded this would be blame for bad luck.

But the excuse has a counterfeit form. Consider a manager who tells his staff that he wants results and does not want to hear how they are achieved. When it later emerges that the results were achieved by falsifying safety records, he can say, truthfully, that he did not know. Yet his not knowing was not something that happened to him; it was something he arranged. He foresaw that there might be facts he would be obliged to act on, and he took steps to ensure that he would never be in possession of them. To accept his excuse would be to reward precisely the foresight that makes him responsible.

The distinction, then, is not between knowing and not knowing but between ignorance a person suffers and ignorance a person cultivates. The cultivated kind does not cancel responsibility; if anything, it adds a second fault to the first, since the person has both allowed a wrong to occur and taken care to shield himself from ever having to confront it. This is why such a person can seem, on reflection, no better and perhaps rather worse than someone who knew the facts plainly and still did nothing about them.

One should not push this too far. No one can investigate everything, and a demand that we trace the full history of every product we buy and every institution we deal with would be impossible to meet and would make ordinary life unworkable. Routine purchases, everyday dealings with banks and employers, and the countless small transactions of daily life proceed, quite properly, on the assumption that things are roughly as they appear. The charge of cultivated ignorance is fair only where a person had some particular reason to suspect a problem and chose not to look because looking might oblige him to act. Without such a reason, not inquiring is simply the normal condition of finite beings, and it is no more blameworthy than failing to read every book in a library.

CAT-VARC-P01-007 · Knowledge and belief · passage Medium · 587 words · 5 questions

Intellectual humility is praised more often than it is defined, and the praise tends to blur it with other things. Sometimes it is equated with low confidence, as if the humble thinker were one who held every opinion loosely. Sometimes it is identified with deference, the willingness to yield to those who know more. And sometimes it means no more than a modest manner, the habit of prefacing one's claims with 'I may be wrong, but'. Each of these captures something, yet none of them can be the virtue itself, because each can be present where humility is absent and absent where humility is present.

Take low confidence first. A person may be unsure of everything out of sheer timidity, believing too little rather than too much, and timidity of this kind is no more admirable than arrogance; it is merely a different way of misjudging one's evidence. Conversely, someone may be entirely certain that the earth orbits the sun, and rightly so, without any failure of humility. Deference fares no better. It is often wise, but a thinker who deferred to every apparent authority would be credulous rather than humble, and one who refused to defer to a genuine expert on a question outside her competence might be guilty of several faults without its being clear that humility is the one she lacks. As for the modest manner, it concerns how one presents oneself to others, and it can coexist with a private conviction that one is never mistaken. The phrase 'I may be wrong' is cheap to say.

A more promising account locates humility in a person's relation to her own limitations. The humble thinker is one who notices the gaps in her knowledge, the places where her reasoning is shaky and the biases to which she is prone, and who takes these into account: she seeks out the evidence she lacks, rechecks the steps she is unsure of, and discounts the conclusions she has special reason to want. This is compatible with great confidence where her grounds are good. Indeed, it is partly because she is alert to where her grounds are poor that her confidence elsewhere deserves to be taken seriously. Her humility, on this view, is not a feeling about herself but a settled pattern of attention and correction, visible chiefly in what she does whenever her own judgement is at stake.

It may be objected that this account makes humility indistinguishable from good reasoning in general. Anyone who reasons well, after all, attends to the strength of her evidence. But the two can come apart. A scientist may be superb at weighing evidence about the world and yet curiously blind to the ways in which her own ambitions shape which questions she asks and which results she finds convincing. Her failure is not a failure to reason about stars or cells; it is a failure to treat herself as one more possible source of error. That reflexive attention, directed at the reasoner rather than at the subject matter, is what the word humility usefully picks out.

The account has a further merit: it explains why humility can be overdone. A person preoccupied with her own limitations, forever cataloguing the ways in which she might be mistaken, may become unable to act on what she knows or to contribute what she has to offer. Owning one's limitations includes keeping them in proportion, and the thinker who is paralysed by self-scrutiny has misjudged them as surely as the one who ignores them.

The method that actually operates is one of mutual adjustment. Principles are revised in the light of judgements about cases, and judgements about cases are revised in the light of principles that have earned our confidence elsewhere. Critics sometimes say that this is circular, and in a sense it is; but the circle is wide, and it is not obvious that any inquiry into how we should live could proceed in a straight line.

What follows is a modest but not trivial conclusion. Intuitions are neither oracles nor noise. The debunker who dismisses them all owes us an account of the standpoint from which the dismissal is made, and it is hard to find one that does not rely on some judgement of the same kind. The defender who treats them as data owes us something too: a willingness to let a strong theory overturn a widely shared reaction, as moral thought has done before, when the reaction turns out to rest on a feature of cases that should never have mattered.

Yet it is hard to describe the abolition of legal slavery, or the end of public torture as a form of entertainment, merely as changes in fashion, as though they were on a par with a shift in the length of coats. Most people who doubt the idea of moral progress in theory find themselves unable to give it up in particular cases.

Progress consists in recognising as morally considerable those whom we previously ignored. The picture is appealing because it gives progress a direction without requiring agreement on a complete moral theory: one needs only the modest premise that arbitrary exclusion is a mistake. It also explains why reformers have so often described their work as opening the eyes of their contemporaries rather than changing their values: the values, on this telling, were already present, and only their application had been blocked.

The picture is not wrong, but it is incomplete in a way that matters. Some of the clearest cases of progress involve not who counts but what we owe to those who already counted. Such corrections can be recognised as improvements without a finished theory of the good, in the way that a scientist can recognise an error without possessing the final truth. That is a more modest claim than the circle image offers, but it is also harder to dismiss as the vanity of the present.

Virtue ethics asks not what rule an action follows or what outcome it produces but what kind of person performs it. Its central concepts are traits of character such as honesty, courage and compassion, conceived as stable dispositions that lead a person to act well across a wide range of circumstances. The compassionate person does not help only when it is convenient or when someone is watching; her compassion shows itself in the market and at home, towards friends and towards strangers. It is this breadth that distinguishes a virtue from a habit, and it is what makes the attribution of a virtue a substantial claim about a person rather than a summary of what she happened to do last week.

In recent decades a group of critics, often called situationists, has argued that this picture is empirically naive. They point to a body of experimental work in social psychology, some of it now disputed, which has been taken to show that small and morally irrelevant features of a situation, such as whether a person is in a hurry or in a pleasant mood, predict whether she will help someone in need far better than anything we know about her character. If behaviour is driven so heavily by circumstance, the critics conclude, then broad traits of the kind virtue ethics requires are rare or nonexistent, and a theory built around them describes a psychology that human beings do not have. On this view, to call someone honest or kind is less a discovery about her than a story we tell to make her conduct look more orderly than it is.

The argument is more damaging to some versions of virtue ethics than to others. If the theory claimed that most people possess the virtues, the evidence would tell heavily against it. But the classical tradition never claimed this. It held that full virtue is difficult, uncommon and achieved, if at all, through long training, and that most people are a mixture of good impulses and weak resolve. Evidence that ordinary people are easily swayed by circumstance is exactly what such a tradition would predict. This reply, however, has a cost that its defenders do not always acknowledge. An ideal that almost no one embodies may still be worth pursuing, but it begins to look less like a description of character and more like a rule of the kind the theory set out to replace, a standard against which conduct is measured rather than a source from which it flows.

The situationist challenge, then, does not refute virtue ethics, but it changes what the theory can coherently promise. The theory can keep its account of the ideal, or its claim to realism about ordinary people, but it is hard to see how it can keep both. Which it should give up is a question that experiments alone cannot answer, since it depends on what we want an ethical theory to do: to guide those who aspire to goodness, or to describe those whose characters are already formed.

On this view a person's liberty is diminished whenever someone interferes with her choices, by force, by threat or by the closing off of options, and it is enlarged whenever such interference is removed. The view has the merit of simplicity, and it gives a clear direction to political argument: other things being equal, less interference means more freedom. An older tradition, recently revived, holds that this account misses something essential. What makes a person unfree, it says, is not interference as such but dependence on the will of another who could interfere at pleasure, whether or not he ever does.

The point is easiest to see in a case where the two accounts come apart. Imagine a tenant whose lease allows the landlord to evict her at any time and for any reason, and whose landlord happens to be a mild man who has never troubled anyone. On the non-interference view the tenant is perfectly free, since nobody is stopping her from doing anything. Yet she knows her position. She will think twice before complaining about a broken boiler, she will keep her opinion of the landlord to herself, and she may find herself anticipating his preferences in matters he has never raised. The absence of interference in her life is not evidence that she is free; it may be, in part, an effect of her unfreedom, since a person who must stay in another's good graces learns to make interference unnecessary. The rival tradition calls her condition domination, and it defines freedom as the absence of domination.

The shift has striking consequences. Interference, on this view, does not always reduce freedom. A law that restrains me, but that I and others like me can contest through courts, elections and other channels that force those who make it to take our interests into account, does not subject me to anyone's arbitrary will, and so does not make me less free, even though it limits what I may do. Egalitarians hold that there is something wrong with inequality as such: that when some people are much worse off than others through no fault of their own, the situation is bad, and bad in part simply because of the gap. The most famous objection to this view asks us to consider what follows from it. Suppose that the only way to remove an inequality were to make the better off worse off, with no benefit to anyone, as if a town in which some households had gardens and others had none could reach equality only by paving over every garden. If inequality is bad in itself, then such a change would remove something bad, and so would be in one respect an improvement. Yet the change helps no one and harms many. To call it an improvement in any respect, the objectors say, is absurd.

Many philosophers have been moved by this objection to give up equality as a fundamental value. Some have adopted the view that benefits matter more the worse off their recipient is, so that a gain to a poor person counts for more than the same gain to a rich one; others hold that what matters is that everyone should have enough, after which differences cease to be of moral concern. Neither view is troubled by levelling down, since on neither does reducing the well-being of the better off, by itself, improve anything. Both also capture much of what drew people to equality in the first place, which was a concern for those who are badly off.

Yet the objection is less decisive than it is usually taken to be. The egalitarian need not claim that a levelled-down world is better all things considered, only that it is better in one respect while much worse in others. Plural values often pull apart in this way. We can think that a promise kept at terrible cost was in one respect admirable without thinking that it should have been kept. Seen this way, the levelling-down case shows only that equality is not the only thing that matters, which almost no egalitarian ever believed.

This defence succeeds, but at a price. It keeps equality as a value only by making it a strangely impersonal one: something that can make a state of affairs better while making it better for no one at all. Many will find such a value hard to care about. There is, however, another way to understand what egalitarians were after. On a relational view, the core of equality lies not in how goods are distributed but in how people stand to one another: in the absence of rank, servility and contempt, in a society where no one must bow and no one may command another merely by virtue of birth or wealth. Inequalities of resources matter on this view chiefly because, beyond some point, they turn into inequalities of standing, as when wealth buys influence over others or poverty forces deference to them.

The relational view is untouched by the levelling-down objection, because paving over gardens neither creates nor abolishes any relation of rank between neighbours. Whether it can say enough about distribution is a further question. A society might abolish titles and deference while leaving vast differences in income intact, and the relational egalitarian owes us an account of when such differences begin to corrode equal standing. But the view has at least located the value of equality in a relation between persons, which is where most of its defenders always felt it to be.

A third appeals to uncertainty: the further away an outcome, the less sure we can be that it will happen at all, or that there will be anyone for it to happen to.

Pure time preference is the weakest of these, at least when it is applied across generations. A person who prefers a pleasure today to a larger one next year may be imprudent, but the impatience is his own and so are its costs. To discount the well-being of people not yet born merely because they will be born later is a different matter: it is to treat a position in time as a reason for counting someone's suffering as less, in just the way that it would be indefensible to count suffering as less because it occurs in a distant country. The growth argument is more respectable, because it does not deny the equal standing of future people; it says only that the same resources will do them less good. But it is respectable only on its own assumptions. It presupposes that the future will in fact be richer, which is precisely what is in doubt if the harm in question is severe enough to undermine prosperity, and it applies only to losses for which wealth can compensate. A flooded coastline or an extinct species is not made good by a larger economy, and to discount such losses at the rate at which consumption grows is to treat them as though they could be bought back.

The argument from uncertainty deserves more sympathy than critics of discounting usually allow it. It is reasonable to give some weight to the possibility that there will be no future people at all, and a small discount for that possibility is defensible. But the rate this argument supports is far lower than the rates commonly used, and it has nothing to say about harms we are confident will occur. What emerges from these distinctions is that the choice of a discount rate is not the technical matter it is often taken to be. Each component encodes a substantive view: about whether time itself diminishes moral standing, about how confident we are in continued growth, about which losses money can repair. A rate presented as a neutral parameter is, in effect, a verdict on what the present owes to the future, reached without the argument such a verdict requires.

A long tradition in the philosophy of science treats explanation and prediction as two faces of one achievement. To explain why an event happened, on this view, is to show that it could have been expected, given the relevant laws and the conditions that held beforehand; to predict an event is to perform the same derivation before the event rather than after it. The symmetry is attractive because it makes explanation answerable to something. An explanation that could not, even in principle, have served as a prediction would seem to be no more than a story told after the fact. The view also promised a single standard for the whole of science, from astronomy, where eclipses are calculated centuries ahead, to clinical medicine, where a diagnosis is expected both to account for a patient's symptoms and to anticipate their course.

Yet the symmetry fails in both directions. A falling barometer reading is an excellent predictor of a coming storm, and anyone who knows the correlation can forecast the weather with it; but no one supposes that the barometer explains the storm. Both are effects of a drop in atmospheric pressure, and the reading earns its predictive power by riding on that common cause. Conversely, we can often explain what we could never have predicted. A geologist can say why a particular hillside gave way, pointing to saturated soil and a weakened layer of clay, without having been able to say beforehand that it would fail that week rather than in some other year. The explanation is sound even though the forecast was out of reach.

What these cases suggest is that explanation and prediction answer to different demands. A predictor need only be reliably connected with what it predicts; it does not matter how the connection arises. An explanation must identify what the outcome depends on: the factors such that, had they been different, the outcome would have been different too. The barometer fails that test, since a broken barometer would not have prevented the storm; the clay passes it, since firmer clay would have held the slope.

None of this makes prediction irrelevant to explanation. A claim about what an outcome depends on usually implies something about what would happen under other conditions, and checking those implications is how such claims are tested. A theory that explains everything and forbids nothing should make us suspicious. But the test is a check on an explanation, not its substance. To confuse the two is to mistake the evidence that an account is right for the thing that makes it an account at all.

The sceptical argument about induction, usually traced to David Hume, has a disarming simplicity. Every inference from observed cases to unobserved ones assumes that the future will resemble the past, or more generally that nature is uniform. That assumption cannot be proved by logic, since its denial involves no contradiction, and it cannot be supported by experience without circularity, since the support would itself be an inference from observed cases. Philosophers have spent a long time looking for a way out, usually by searching for a single principle that would license inductive inference in general.

The search may be misconceived, because there is no such thing as inductive inference in general. Consider two investigators. One measures the melting point of a newly prepared sample of a pure compound and concludes, from a single careful trial, that every sample of that compound will melt at the same temperature. Another measures the height of one adult and draws no conclusion at all about the heights of adults. Both are reasoning well, and they differ not because one has found the universal principle and the other has not, but because each knows something about the kind of thing being studied: that pure compounds are uniform in their melting points, and that people are not uniform in their heights.

On this picture, what licenses an inductive step is not a principle of uniformity but particular facts about particular domains, which tell us how far a sample can be trusted to stand in for the whole. The obvious objection is that this merely relocates the problem. Our confidence that compounds are uniform in their melting points was itself acquired by induction, and so the sceptic can ask what licenses that.

The objection is fair, but it is less damaging than it looks, because it trades on an ambiguity between justifying each belief and justifying all of them together. The regress it describes is real, yet it never requires us to justify all our inductive habits at once. At any moment we lean on some background knowledge in order to test some other part of it, and any item can in turn be questioned while the rest is held fixed. This does not answer the sceptic on the sceptic's own terms, which demand a foundation lying outside experience altogether. It does show that the demand is not one that science, as actually practised, needs to meet in order to be rational, any more than a household needs to audit every account on the same afternoon in order to keep honest books.

Faced with two theories that fit the evidence equally well, scientists usually prefer the simpler, and they rarely feel the need to say why. When pressed, they tend to offer one of two answers. The first is that simplicity is a convenience: simpler theories are easier to state, to teach and to calculate with, and so they deserve priority in the way that a tidy desk deserves priority over a cluttered one, without any suggestion that tidiness is truer. A more promising answer comes from an unglamorous source, the practice of fitting curves to data. Additional adjustable parameters let a model accommodate the accidents of a particular sample, and the price of that accommodation is paid when the model meets new data. Seen in this light, a preference for simplicity is a policy for estimating well from limited and imperfect evidence, not a belief about the architecture of the world.

This answer has a cost that its admirers do not always acknowledge. The statistical argument shows that, in the situations scientists usually face, favouring the simpler of two well-fitting hypotheses is a reliable way to be less wrong. Values may legitimately decide which problems are worth studying and how results are used, but in the central act of science, deciding whether the evidence supports a hypothesis, they are supposed to have no place. The trouble is that evidence rarely settles a question outright, and so accepting a hypothesis always involves deciding that the evidence is strong enough. How strong is strong enough? Which error is worse, and therefore how much evidence to demand before declaring the solvent harmful, is not something the data can decide. It depends on how one weighs injured workers against lost revenue, which is a question of value. Two laboratories examining identical data could therefore set the threshold in different places, and neither would have misread a single measurement.

A natural reply is that scientists need not accept or reject anything. They could report the probability that the hypothesis is true, given the evidence, and leave the decision to those who must act. Thought experiments occupy an awkward place in the theory of knowledge. Scientists and philosophers have long used them to settle questions, and some of the most admired episodes in the history of physics turn on an imagined scenario rather than an actual measurement. Yet a thought experiment, by definition, collects no new data. The experimenter sits still and imagines. If we learn something about the world this way, it is not obvious where the information comes from, and two answers have dominated discussion.

According to the first, a thought experiment is an argument in disguise. Its vivid scenario can always be rewritten as a set of premises and a conclusion, and whatever it teaches, it teaches because the premises were already believed and the conclusion follows from them. The imagery is persuasive packaging, useful perhaps for teaching, but adding nothing to the logical content. According to the second, the best thought experiments give us something no argument could: a kind of direct intellectual insight into the necessary structure of nature, as if the mind could see a law of physics in the way the eye sees a colour.

Neither answer fits the most celebrated example well. Galileo asked his readers to imagine a heavy body and a light one tied together and dropped. If heavier bodies fall faster, as the received physics of his day held, the light body should hold back the heavy one, so that the pair falls more slowly than the heavy body alone; but the pair is heavier than the heavy body, so it should fall faster. The supposition contradicts itself, and the natural way out is that all bodies fall at the same rate, at least in the absence of a resisting medium. The argument reading can reconstruct this, but only by writing in premises that no one had formulated before the scenario was imagined: for instance, that the speed of a composite body must lie somewhere between the speeds of its parts. The insight reading, for its part, makes the result mysterious, as though Galileo had glimpsed a law rather than noticed a contradiction in what he and his readers already believed.

A better account is that thought experiments work by drawing on knowledge we have but have never made explicit. Everyone who has carried a load or watched things fall has a large stock of tacit expectations about how bodies behave. A well-constructed scenario brings these expectations to bear on a case where they collide with an explicit theory, and so exposes a tension that was there all along. What is new is not the information but its arrangement. This explains why thought experiments can be genuinely informative without new data, and also why they cannot be reduced to an argument that someone might have written down beforehand: the premises are found by running the scenario, not before it.

It also explains when they should not be trusted. Our tacit expectations were formed in a narrow band of experience, among objects of moderate size moving at moderate speeds. A thought experiment that asks us to imagine the very small, the very fast or the very distant invites us to apply those expectations where they were never tested, and the resulting conviction, however vivid, carries only the authority of the experience behind it. The method is neither a trick of rhetoric nor a window onto necessity. It is a way of interrogating what we already know, and it is exactly as reliable as that knowledge.

Statements of chance are among the most familiar in science and ordinary life, and among the hardest to interpret. When we say that a fair coin has a chance of one half of landing heads, what are we saying? The most influential answer identifies chance with frequency: the chance of heads is the proportion of heads in a long run of tosses, or the limit towards which that proportion tends as the run is extended without end. The answer has the great merit of tying chance to something that can be counted, and it explains why observed frequencies are our main evidence about chances.

Its difficulties begin with the single case. A particular coin may be tossed once and then melted down; a particular patient either will or will not recover from a particular operation. Yet we speak readily of the chance that this toss lands heads or that this patient recovers, and such judgements guide real decisions. The frequency view can treat them only by placing the single case in a class of similar cases and borrowing the frequency of the class. And here a second difficulty appears, because every case belongs to many classes. The patient is a man of sixty, a non-smoker, a resident of a particular city, someone treated by a particular surgeon, and the recovery rates in these classes may differ considerably. Which frequency is the patient's chance?

Dissatisfaction with this reference class problem has led many to prefer a propensity view, on which chance is a physical tendency of a particular set-up to produce a given outcome, a property the single toss or the single operation possesses whether or not any long run ever occurs. The propensity view handles the single case gracefully, but at a price. It is unclear what a propensity is, beyond a name for whatever it is that frequencies are evidence of, and the view does not escape the problem it was meant to solve. The tendency of a set-up depends on how the set-up is described: the toss described as a toss of a fair coin has one propensity, while the toss described down to the exact force and angle of the flick may have another, close to certainty.

The persistence of the problem across both views suggests that it is not a defect peculiar to either but a feature of chance itself. A chance is not a property an event has simply, but a property it has relative to a specification of the circumstances. Asking for the chance of an event, full stop, is like asking for the speed of a train without saying relative to what: the question is not unanswerable but incomplete. What remains open, and must be settled by our purposes rather than by the world, is which specification to use. For a gambler who cannot measure the flick, the description 'fair coin' is the relevant one; for a physicist with a high-speed camera, it is not.

Some will feel that this concedes too much, making chance a matter of perspective rather than a feature of the world. But relativity to a description is not the same as subjectivity. Once the specification is fixed, the chance is fixed too, and two observers who adopt the same description and disagree about the chance cannot both be right. Velocity is no less objective for being relative to a frame. What the account gives up is only the idea that there is a single privileged description, the one nature itself would choose, and it is far from clear that anything in science requires such a description.

The teaching has been admired across many centuries and many traditions, and it has also been accused of something close to moral recklessness. An agent who does not care about outcomes, the objection runs, is an agent who will not count the cost of what he does.

The objection rests on running together two different things: attending to consequences and depending on them. Nothing in the idea of acting without attachment forbids a person from considering what his action is likely to bring about; a physician who acts in this spirit still chooses the treatment most likely to help. What the teaching asks is that the physician's steadiness, and his sense of having done what he should, not be held hostage to whether the patient recovers. Consequences enter into deciding what to do. They do not enter into the agent's claim to a reward, or into the anxious craving that so often bends judgement toward whatever promises to be noticed and praised.

Seen this way, detachment is less a withdrawal from the world than a condition for acting well in it. Anxiety about results is notoriously bad for performance; the craftsman watching the clock and the speaker imagining the applause both tend to do worse work than the one absorbed in the task. More seriously, an agent fixed on fruits is tempted to choose the action whose success will be visible over the one that is right, and to abandon a just course the moment it begins to fail. The teaching guards against both distortions.

It would be too quick, however, to declare the tension dissolved. Consequences cannot be considered in choosing and then set aside entirely in acting, because most actions unfold over time and must be adjusted as their effects appear. The physician has to watch whether the treatment is working. Whether that relation can be sustained by ordinary people under real pressure is a question the teaching poses rather than settles.

In ordinary speech leisure is what is left over when work is done. It is time off, and its value is usually explained by reference to the work it interrupts: we rest so that we can return refreshed, and an employer who grants generous holidays is said to be investing in productivity. Aristotle saw the relation the other way round. In the Nicomachean Ethics he argues that we are busy in order to have leisure, much as we make war in order to have peace, and the Greek word for leisure, schole, is the ancestor of our word 'school'. Leisure in his sense was not rest or idleness. It was the condition of being free to engage in activities chosen for their own sake, of which he held contemplation to be the highest, though he also counted the cultivated enjoyment of music among its proper occupations. Amusement, by contrast, belonged with work. We need relaxation because we cannot labour continuously, and amusement provides it, so amusement exists for the sake of further activity rather than as an end in itself; to toil for the sake of amusement struck him as childish.

The modern inversion has a consequence that is easy to miss. Once leisure is understood as recovery for the sake of work, it inherits the standards of work. Free time becomes something to be used well, which increasingly means used productively: hobbies are pursued for the skills they build, exercise for the stamina it sustains, even sleep for its effect on the next day's performance. The result is not merely that people have too little time off, though many do, but that the time they have is colonised by the logic it was supposed to interrupt. Aristotle would have recognised the pattern. A life organised entirely around work and the recovery it requires has, in his terms, no leisure at all, however many holidays it contains.

The obvious objection is that Aristotle's leisure was a privilege resting on the labour of others. The citizens free for philosophy and politics were supported by slaves, by women whose sphere he confined to the household, and by labourers and artisans whom he considered unsuited to full citizenship in the best city. A conception of the good life that excludes most people from it can hardly serve as a model for a society in which nearly everyone must work. This objection is just, and it should not be softened. But it tells against the distribution of leisure in Aristotle's city, not against his account of what leisure is. One can reject the arrangement that gave a few men freedom from necessity while still holding that freedom from necessity is valuable for what it makes possible, and that what it makes possible is not simply more work.

What follows for us is less a programme than a question. If the value of leisure lies in activities worth doing for their own sake, then the measure of a society's free time is not only how much of it there is but whether people have the means, the education and the settled habits to fill it with such activities, rather than with recovery alone. That is a demand on schools and cities as much as on employers, and it is one that the language of rest and productivity has no words to state.

Few doctrines have been more often misunderstood by sympathetic outsiders than the Buddhist teaching of anatta, usually translated as 'no-self'. Stated baldly, as the claim that there is no self, it seems to invite an easy refutation: someone, after all, is entertaining the doctrine, and someone will be disappointed if it turns out to be false. Critics who press this point imagine that they have found a contradiction at the heart of the tradition. In fact they have mostly found a problem of translation, and behind it a mistaken assumption about what kind of claim the teaching was meant to be.

In the early discourses the teaching typically appears not as a thesis about what exists but as an exercise. The listener is led through the components that make up a person, which the tradition groups as bodily form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness, and is asked of each whether it is permanent, whether it is reliably satisfying and whether it is under one's control. Since none of them passes these tests, none, the discourse concludes, should be regarded as 'mine', as what 'I am', or as 'my self'. The point of the exercise is practical. According to the tradition's own account of suffering, its origin lies in craving, and craving is sustained by the habit of treating changing and uncontrollable processes as possessions one must defend. To see that nothing in experience is fit to be owned in that way is to loosen the grip of the habit.

Read in this light, the easy refutation misses its target. The teaching does not deny that there is a stream of experiences, nor that the stream has a history, makes choices and bears their consequences; the same tradition has a great deal to say about all three. What it denies is that there is, in addition to the stream, an unchanging owner standing behind it, and it denies this chiefly because belief in such an owner is what makes us cling. A later dialogue makes the point with a chariot: the word names an assembly of axle, wheels and frame, and nothing is lost by admitting that there is no further chariot over and above the parts, arranged as they are. The person, likewise, is a convenient name for a functioning whole, not a hidden thing to which the whole belongs.

It would be too convenient, however, to conclude that the doctrine is purely therapeutic and therefore immune to philosophical scrutiny. A therapy that works by inducing a belief earns its authority only if the belief is warranted; otherwise it is a comforting fiction, and the tradition has never presented its teaching as a fiction. The exercise succeeds, by its own standards, because it reveals how things are. So the metaphysical question cannot be set aside; it can only be stated more precisely. The question is not whether anyone exists but whether a person is anything more than the organised stream, and on that question the doctrine takes a position that some philosophers in other traditions have also defended, and others have rejected, without anyone having settled the matter.

The lesson for the sympathetic outsider is therefore double. The easy refutation fails, because it attacks a claim the teaching does not make. But the easy defence fails too, because the teaching is not merely a technique; it is a technique whose authority rests on a claim about the self that may be true or false, and that deserves to be argued about on its merits rather than admired from a respectful distance.
