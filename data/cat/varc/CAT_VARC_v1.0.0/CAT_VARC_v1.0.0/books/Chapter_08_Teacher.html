CAT-VARC-P08-001 · Language and linguistics · passage Easy · 458 words · 4 questions

In a number of countries a newborn cannot simply be given whatever name the parents choose. A registrar may refuse a name that is judged offensive, that does not clearly indicate whether the bearer is male or female, that is spelt in a way the national orthography does not allow, or that does not appear on an official list. Defenders of such rules usually present them as a modest form of child protection. Parents, the argument runs, are fallible and sometimes vain; the child carries the name for a lifetime and had no say in choosing it; and the state, which already requires that children be schooled and vaccinated, may reasonably insist that they not be saddled with a label that will invite mockery.

There is something to this. A name is not a private ornament but a tool that other people use, and a name that cannot be pronounced, written on a form or read out in a classroom without laughter does impose a cost on the person who bears it. Cases in which parents have tried to register a brand name, a string of numbers or an insult are real enough, and few people would object to a registrar declining them. If the rules went no further than this, they would hardly be worth arguing about.

But they usually go much further, and the further they go, the less work the child-protection argument does. A rule that a name must indicate the bearer's sex protects no child from ridicule; it protects a social expectation. A rule that a name may use only the letters of the national alphabet does not shield the daughter of immigrant parents from mockery; if anything, it obliges her to carry an altered spelling of her grandmother's name, one that marks her at every roll call as someone whose family had to be corrected. An approved list, finally, protects children chiefly from novelty, and novelty is not a harm. Many names now regarded as solid and traditional were once eccentric inventions, and a register that had frozen the stock of names a century ago would have prevented most of them.

What the stricter rules actually defend, then, is the stock of names itself, treated as a piece of national heritage that private choices might erode. That is a coherent aim, and some will think it a worthy one. It should, however, be argued for openly as cultural policy and weighed against the interest parents have in naming their children as their own traditions require, rather than smuggled in under the language of protecting the young. A state that wishes to guard children from cruel names needs a short list of what is forbidden, not a long list of what is allowed.

CAT-VARC-P08-002 · Language and linguistics · passage Medium · 459 words · 4 questions

The quarrel between those who lay down rules for English and those who merely record how it is spoken is usually staged as a contest between snobbery and science. On one side stand the guardians of usage, who object to split infinitives and sentence-final prepositions and who regard each new sense of an old word as a small act of vandalism. On the other stand the linguists, who point out that many of the rules are of recent and dubious origin, that admired writers have broken them for centuries, and that a grammar describing what speakers actually do is the only kind that deserves the name. Put this way, the linguists win easily, and in the universities, at least, they have been winning for a long time.

Yet the victory is less complete than it appears, because it rests on a confusion about what a rule of usage is. The linguist is right that no rule of this kind is a law of the language in the way that, say, the order of article and noun is: speakers who break it are still perfectly understood, and they are still speaking English. But it does not follow that such rules are mere prejudice. Beliefs about correctness are themselves part of the linguistic behaviour of a community. A speaker who avoids a construction in a job application while using it freely at home is not confused; she is responding accurately to a social fact, namely that certain readers will judge her by it. A descriptive grammar that ignored such judgements would be describing an imaginary community.

What the prescriptivist gets wrong is not the existence of the rules but their source. The traditional defence appeals to logic, or to the example of Latin, or to a clarity the condemned construction supposedly lacks. These appeals rarely survive inspection. The objection to splitting an infinitive, for instance, makes some sense in a language where the infinitive is a single word and cannot be split, and very little in one where it is two. The real authority of such a rule is conventional: it binds because enough educated readers expect it to be observed, and for no deeper reason.

Seeing the rules as conventions of a register, rather than as laws or as prejudices, changes the question we should ask about them. It is no longer whether a usage is correct in some absolute sense, but whether a given convention earns its keep: whether it marks a distinction readers find useful, or merely sets a test that excludes those who were never taught it. Some conventions pass that test; many fail it. The point is that they can be judged, and that judging them is neither the snob's work nor the scientist's alone.

CAT-VARC-P08-003 · Language and linguistics · passage Medium · 448 words · 4 questions

A parent who hears a child begin a sentence in one language and finish it in another often worries that the child has learned neither properly. Teachers have voiced the same anxiety, and so, at times, have bilingual speakers themselves, who describe their own mixing with a kind of apology, as though it betrayed a laziness they ought to overcome. The assumption behind the worry is simple: a speaker who switches must be reaching for a word he does not have, and a speaker who is constantly reaching has not finished learning.

The assumption is not absurd, and sometimes it is true. Anyone who has lived between two languages knows the moment when the word for a kitchen utensil, or for a form at the bank, has been acquired in one language and simply not in the other. But careful recordings of bilingual conversation suggest that such gaps account for only part of what happens, and not the most interesting part. Switches tend to fall at points where the grammars of the two languages fit together and to avoid points where they clash, so that a sentence begun in one language and finished in another is usually well formed by the standards of both. A speaker who was merely groping for words would not be so tidy. To switch in this way, one needs to know two grammars well enough to sense, without thinking, where they can be joined.

Nor is the timing of a switch random. Speakers move into the other language to report what someone else said, to mark a change of topic, to soften a refusal, to signal that a remark is a joke, or to draw a listener in or shut one out. A grandmother's proverb may be quoted in the language she used, not because the speaker cannot translate it, but because the translation would lose the grandmother. In such uses the switch itself carries meaning, much as a change of tone does within a single language; it is a resource that monolingual speakers lack, not a failure that they have avoided.

None of this means that every setting must welcome mixing. An examination set in one language, or a document that must be read by people who know only one, may reasonably require a speaker to stay within it, and a bilingual who could not do so would indeed have a problem. The error lies in treating the ability to keep languages apart as the measure of competence, and the habit of combining them as its absence. Most bilinguals can do both. Judging them by the first alone is like judging a pianist only by whether she can play with one hand.

CAT-VARC-P08-004 · Language and linguistics · passage Easy · 422 words · 4 questions

Few arguments about words are made with more confidence than the appeal to origins. Someone uses 'decimate' to mean 'destroy most of', and a correspondent points out that the word comes from a Roman military punishment in which one soldier in ten was put to death, so that its proper sense is the loss of a tenth. Someone else describes an idea as 'nice' and is reminded that the word once meant 'foolish' or 'ignorant'. The assumption in each case is that a word's earliest meaning is its real one, and that later senses are errors which happen to have spread.

The assumption cannot be applied consistently, and nobody tries. If original meanings were binding, 'nice' would still be an insult, and so would a good many words now used without a second thought; the critic who insists on the Roman sense of 'decimate' does not, as a rule, insist on the earlier senses of the other words in his own sentence. Nor is there any principled place to stop. Most words have a history that runs back past the language in which they are now used, and the 'original' sense of any word is simply the earliest one of which we happen to have records. To treat that sense as authoritative is to give a vote to speakers who have been dead for centuries and to deny one to everyone now alive.

What a word means is settled, as far as anything settles it, by how the people who use it understand one another. This is not a counsel of indifference, for a shift in meaning can have a cost: if a word that once marked a fine distinction comes to be used loosely, the distinction may become harder to express, and a writer may reasonably prefer to keep it. But that is an argument about present usefulness, and it has to be made on those terms. It gains nothing from the fact that the stricter sense came first.

Etymology is not thereby made worthless. The history of a word can explain an odd spelling, reveal a forgotten link between two words that now look unrelated, or show how a community's way of life left its traces in the vocabulary, and it is one of the pleasures of knowing a language well; what it cannot do is serve as a court of appeal. To know where a word came from is to know something about the past of a language; it is not to know what the word must mean today.

The image is understandable, since a dictionary speaks in the flat, confident voice of a statute, but it misrepresents how the better dictionaries are made. A serious dictionary does not decide what words mean; it reports, as carefully and as comprehensively as it can, what speakers and writers have been observed to mean by them.

In at least one influential strand of its history, objectivity was proposed not as a description of the journalist but as a remedy for the journalist's limitations. Precisely because reporters could not be trusted to be neutral, the argument ran, they needed a method that would restrain what their partiality could do to the record.

The method in question resembled, in a modest way, the procedures of empirical inquiry. A claim was to be checked against more than one source; documents were to be preferred to recollection; and the reporter was to say where each piece of information came from, so that a reader or a rival could test it. On this understanding the objectivity belonged to the procedure, not to the person, much as a laboratory's results are trusted not because its scientists lack ambitions but because their work is arranged so that others can repeat it. A reporter with strong opinions could practise the method; indeed, the method existed for such reporters, since a reporter without opinions, if one could be found, would hardly need it.

What happened over the following decades was a quiet substitution. The first, familiar from introductory logic, holds that an argument stands or falls on its own terms. Who makes it is irrelevant; to reject a claim because of the person advancing it is to commit a fallacy, and to accept a claim because of the person is the same error in a friendlier mood. The second attitude is older. Aristotle counted the character of the speaker among the means of persuasion, alongside appeals to the emotions of the audience and to the argument itself, and he did not present it as a regrettable weakness of listeners. A speaker who seems sensible, honest and well disposed towards the audience is, on this view, more worthy of belief, and an audience is right to take that into account.

The logician's attitude is correct for a particular situation: one in which the listener can examine the argument fully. A proof in geometry is no better for coming from a famous mathematician.   2. (B)regarding them as technical difficulties that better measurement will soon be able to overcome.
  3. (C)treating them as decisive reasons for continuing to rank feeds by momentary reaction.
  4. (D)dismissing them as excuses put forward by those who build ranking systems and profit from them.
Answer and explanation

Answer: (A)

Paragraph 5 grants that the objections are 'real' and 'counsel modesty about any redesign', but adds 'they cut both ways', since a reaction-based system has 'also made a judgement about what its users really want'. That is acceptance as caution combined with a refusal to let them favour the status quo.

Why not (B): The author predicts no technical solution; the objections include a charge of substituted judgement, which better measurement would not remove.

Why not (C): The author says the objections 'cut both ways': a reaction-based system also makes a silent judgement, so they are not reasons to keep it.

Why not (D): The author calls them 'real objections' that 'counsel modesty', which is not dismissal, and does not attribute them to builders.

Common misconception: Reading the concession as an endorsement of the status quo, missing 'they cut both ways'.

Difficulty: The tone combines a genuine concession with a reversal in the last three sentences; two wrong options each capture only one movement. · Exams: CAT, XAT
