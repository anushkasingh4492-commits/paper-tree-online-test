CAT-VARC-C12-001 · Sentence placement · EasyMCQ
There is a sentence that is missing in the paragraph below. Look at the paragraph and decide in which blank (1, 2, 3 or 4) the missing sentence can best fit.
Missing sentence: To forgive, by contrast, is to accept that the wrong was real and was theirs, and then to give up the resentment one is entitled to feel.

Forgiveness is often confused with excusing, but the two are closer to opposites than to near relations. (1) To excuse someone is to decide that what they did was not really their fault: they were tired, misled or under pressure, and so there is nothing to hold against them. (2) This is why forgiveness can be offered only by someone who has been wronged, and why it can cost her something: the resentment she surrenders was hers by right. An excuse costs nothing of the kind, since it removes the very ground of complaint. (3) It follows that the more readily we excuse, the less there is left for us to forgive. A person who explains away every injury may seem generous, but he has quietly declined to treat others as responsible for what they do. (4)

  1. (A)Blank (1)
  2. (B)Blank (2)
  3. (C)Blank (3)
  4. (D)Blank (4)
Answer and explanation

Answer: (B)

The missing sentence defines forgiving 'by contrast' with excusing, so it must follow the account of excusing. It must also come before 'This is why forgiveness can be offered only by someone who has been wronged', since that claim, and the 'resentment she surrenders', follow from forgiveness accepting that 'the wrong was real' and giving up resentment. Only gap (2) lies between the two.

Why not (A): At gap (1) excusing has not yet been explained, so 'by contrast' has nothing to contrast with, and the sentence about excusing would then be followed by a claim about forgiveness that does not follow from it.

Why not (C): At gap (3) the paragraph has already drawn the consequences of forgiving, including the surrendered resentment, so supplying the definition now comes after its use and merely repeats what has been said.

Why not (D): At gap (4) the paragraph has reached its conclusion about the person who excuses everything; defining forgiveness at the end leaves the earlier 'This is why' without its basis.

Common misconception: Placing the definition at gap (3), next to the remark that an excuse costs nothing, because both sentences compare excusing with forgiving.

Difficulty: A contrast marker and the following 'This is why' fix one position; the other gaps are clearly off. · Exams: CAT, XAT, NMAT, SNAP, CMAT, MAH-CET

(1) What makes the error puzzling is not its existence but its persistence, since experience ought to correct it. (2) The usual explanation is that planners take an inside view: they picture the particular steps of the task in front of them and add up the time each step seems to need. Such a picture is built out of intentions, and intentions contain no interruptions. (3) The proposed remedy is to take an outside view, setting aside the details of the project and asking how long projects of the same general kind have actually taken. (4) This feels like throwing information away, because it ignores everything the planner knows about her own case.   1. (A)Blank (1)
  2. (B)Blank (2)
  3. (C)Blank (3)
  4. (D)Blank (4)
Answer and explanation

Answer: (C)

The pronoun 'them' in the missing sentence needs plural interruptions to refer to, and 'by contrast' sets real projects against something that lacks interruptions. Both point to the sentence ending 'intentions contain no interruptions'. The sentence must also come before the remedy, which answers exactly this gap between plan and reality, so it belongs at gap (3).

Why not (A): At gap (1) neither the inside view nor interruptions have been mentioned, so 'them' has no referent and 'by contrast' has nothing to contrast with.

Why not (B): At gap (2) the inside view has not yet been described and interruptions have not been mentioned, so 'them' has nothing to refer to and 'by contrast' sets real projects against nothing.

Why not (D): At gap (4) the remedy has already been proposed; the sentence would cut 'This feels like throwing information away' off from the remedy that 'This' refers to, and 'them' would reach back past the remedy to interruptions two sentences earlier.

Common misconception: Placing the sentence at gap (4), as if real projects were being contrasted with the outside view rather than with intentions.

(2) Because a lichen has no roots and no protective outer skin, it takes in water and nutrients directly from rain and from the air around it.   2. (B)Blank (2)
  3. (C)Blank (3)
  4. (D)Blank (4)

'The very openness' refers back to the description of a lichen taking in water and nutrients directly from rain and air, and 'Yet' turns that strength into a weakness. The weakness must be stated before the evidence that species vanish from polluted city centres. Only gap (3) lies between the two sentences.

Why not (B): At gap (2) the hardiness has been described, but the openness, the absence of roots and skin, comes only in the next sentence, so 'the very openness' has no referent.

CAT-VARC-C12-007 · Sentence placement · DifficultMCQ

Two drivers take the same corner too fast on the same wet evening, and both are equally careless. One reaches home without incident; a child steps into the road in front of the other. We blame the second driver far more severely, and so, in all likelihood, does he. (1) One response is to call our reaction an error: the two are equally guilty, and the second is merely unluckier. (2) This keeps the principle of control intact, but at a cost, for it asks us to treat the grief and remorse of the second driver as a kind of confusion. (3) Another response accepts that luck shapes what we are answerable for, and concludes that the principle was never as firm as it seemed. Neither response is comfortable, and the discomfort is itself the discovery: our practice of blame rests on two commitments that do not fit together. (4)

  1. (A)Blank (1)
  2. (B)Blank (2)
  3. (C)Blank (3)
  4. (D)Blank (4)
Answer and explanation

Answer: (A)

The missing sentence states the puzzle: our harsher blame of the second driver ('this difference') clashes with the idea that blame tracks only control. It must follow the observation that we blame him more, and it must come before 'One response', which needs a problem to respond to, and before 'the principle of control', which the paragraph treats as already introduced. Only gap (1) satisfies both links.

Why not (B): At gap (2) the first response has already declared our reaction an error, so raising the puzzle now is backwards, and it separates 'This keeps the principle of control intact' from the response that 'This' refers to.

Why not (C): At gap (3) the paragraph has already explained what the first response costs; restating the puzzle here interrupts the pairing of the two responses and leaves 'One response' earlier with nothing to respond to.

Why not (D): At gap (4) the paragraph has already concluded that the two commitments do not fit together; posing the puzzle after its own resolution reverses the argument.

Common misconception: Placing the sentence at gap (2), next to 'the principle of control', because it mentions control.

Difficulty: The missing sentence shares the word 'control' with a later sentence, which pulls weaker solvers to gap (2); the correct gap follows from seeing that 'One response' and 'the principle of control' both presuppose the puzzle. · Exams: CAT, XAT
