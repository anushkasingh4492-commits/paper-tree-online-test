When a trait is described as highly heritable, many readers hear a claim about destiny: if height or some aspect of temperament is 'mostly genetic', then upbringing can do little to alter it. The inference feels natural, but it rests on a misunderstanding of what heritability measures. Heritability is a statistic about a population, not about a person. It estimates what share of the variation in a trait, across a particular group living in a particular range of environments, can be statistically associated with genetic differences among the members of that group. It says nothing about how much of any one individual's height was produced by genes, a question that is not even coherent, since every centimetre of a person's height required both genes and food.

Two consequences follow, and both tend to surprise people. First, heritability depends on the environment in which it is measured. If every child in a group were given exactly the same diet, schooling and care, the remaining differences among them would be largely genetic, and the heritability of almost any trait would rise, not because genes had become more powerful but because the environmental differences had been taken away. Conversely, in a group where some children are well fed and others go hungry, differences in nutrition will account for much of the variation in height, and the measured heritability will fall. Second, a trait can be built almost entirely by genes and still show low heritability. Nearly everyone is born with five fingers on each hand because a developmental programme laid down in the genome builds them; most people who have fewer have lost them through injury. Variation in finger number is therefore mainly environmental, even though the fingers themselves are anything but.

The most consequential error concerns differences between groups. Suppose a trait is highly heritable within each of two populations. It is tempting to conclude that the average difference between the populations must also be largely genetic. It need not be. Picture two handfuls of seed taken from the same genetically varied sack, one sown in rich soil and the other in poor soil. Within each plot, differences in height will be largely genetic, since the soil is uniform; between the plots, the difference is due entirely to the soil. High heritability within groups is compatible with a gap between them that has no genetic basis at all.

None of this makes heritability useless. Breeders rely on it to predict how quickly a herd will respond to selection, and researchers use it to judge where genetic studies are likely to find something. The fault lies not with the statistic but with the habit of reading it as a measure of fixity. A highly heritable trait may still be changed by changing the environment; short sight is substantially heritable, and spectacles correct it all the same.

For anyone who thinks of natural selection as a relentless editor of waste, the tail of a peacock is an embarrassment, since it is heavy, conspicuous and awkward in flight, and it makes its bearer easier for predators to spot and harder for him to escape. Darwin treated such ornaments as a problem of their own, and proposed that they spread not because they helped their owners survive but because they helped them mate: females preferred them, and the preference was enough to carry the trait forward. That answer, however, raised a second and more awkward question, namely why females should come to prefer an ornament that makes their mates less likely to survive.

One influential reply turns the embarrassment into the explanation. An ornament can work as an honest signal precisely because it is costly. A male in poor condition cannot afford to grow and carry an extravagant tail; only a male with ample reserves, and perhaps with sound inherited qualities, can bear the burden and still thrive. A female who chooses the most lavish display is therefore choosing, without knowing it, the male best able to pay. On this view the cost is not a side effect to be minimised but the very thing that keeps the signal reliable. A cheap signal could be produced by anyone, and a signal that anyone can produce soon carries no information.

The idea has been extended well beyond courtship. Some antelope, on spotting a predator, leap high into the air on stiff legs instead of fleeing at once, which looks like a waste of precious seconds. If the leap advertises that the animal is fit enough to outrun any chase, a predator that heeds it saves itself a fruitless pursuit, and the leaper is spared an attack. Here too, the display persuades because it could not be produced by an animal that lacked the quality it advertises.

It would be a mistake, though, to treat costliness as the whole story. Theoretical work suggests that what matters is not that a signal is expensive in absolute terms but that it is more expensive for weak individuals than for strong ones; a signal equally costly for all would sort no one. Nor need every ornament be honest. Some preferences may persist simply because females who hold them have sons who inherit the admired trait, a self-reinforcing loop that can carry ornaments well beyond any useful information. The costly signal, in short, explains why many displays can be trusted, not why every display exists.

A familiar complaint holds that the theory of natural selection is empty at its core. Its slogan, survival of the fittest, seems to define the fittest as those who survive, so that the principle reduces to the statement that survivors survive. A claim that cannot be false, the complaint continues, explains nothing; it merely relabels what happened. The objection is old, and it has a certain surface appeal, but it depends on running together two different things: the record of which organisms in fact left offspring and the properties that made them likely to do so.

Consider two moths of the same species, one pale and one dark, resting on bark darkened by soot. The dark moth is fitter in that setting, but not because it happens to have left more offspring. It is fitter because its colouring makes it harder for birds to see, and that is a fact one can establish independently, by measuring how often birds find each kind of moth on each kind of bark. Fitness, properly understood, is a propensity: an expected rate of reproduction given an organism's traits and its surroundings. Like the fragility of a glass, it is a disposition that holds whether or not it is ever displayed. A fragile glass may survive a lifetime on a shelf, and a well-camouflaged moth may die in an unlucky accident; neither fact refutes the disposition.

Seen this way, the theory makes claims that could turn out false. It predicts that when the propensities of variants differ, and the differences are heritable, the frequencies of the variants will shift in a direction one can anticipate from an engineering or ecological analysis of the traits. Such predictions are tested constantly, and some fail, which is precisely what a tautology could never do. The distinction also explains why biologists speak of drift: in small populations the lucky can outbreed the fit, and the actual record of reproduction can depart from the expected one. If fitness simply meant survival, drift would be unintelligible, since there would be no gap between expectation and outcome for chance to fill.

The critics are not wholly wrong, however. Biologists often measure fitness by counting offspring, because propensities are hard to observe directly, and careless writing can slide from the measurement to the definition. The remedy is not to abandon the concept but to keep the evidence for a disposition distinct from the disposition itself.

The sterile worker castes of ants, bees and wasps troubled Darwin as much as almost any feature of the living world. A creature that never reproduces cannot pass on whatever traits make it a good worker, so it was hard to see how natural selection could have refined the elaborate behaviour of workers over many generations. Darwin's tentative answer was that selection might act on the family rather than on the individual, much as a breeder who wants a good strain of cattle keeps the relatives of an animal whose worth became clear only after it was slaughtered.

The idea was given a precise form only in the twentieth century. A gene that disposes its bearer to help relatives can spread even if the bearer leaves no offspring of its own, provided the help raises the reproduction of relatives who are likely to carry copies of the same gene. What matters is the product of two quantities, the benefit to the relative and the probability that the relative shares the gene, weighed against the cost to the helper. Sterility, on this account, is not an anomaly to be explained away but an extreme point on a continuum of helping, reached when a helper can do more for its genes by raising siblings than by raising young of its own.

For a time, a further detail seemed to make the case almost too neat. In ants, bees and wasps, males develop from unfertilised eggs, and a peculiarity of inheritance follows: sisters who share both parents are, on average, more closely related to one another than a mother is to her daughters. If workers are more closely related to their sisters than to their own potential offspring, raising sisters is the better genetic bargain, and sterile castes should arise readily in exactly these insects. The argument was elegant, and it was widely taught. It has not survived well. Termites, whose inheritance has no such peculiarity, have sterile castes too; the queens of many ants and bees mate with several males, which dilutes the relatedness among their daughters; and the great majority of species with this peculiar inheritance are not social at all.

The lesson is not that kinship is irrelevant. Workers in social insect colonies typically do raise relatives, and the general principle remains the best account of why they can afford to forgo reproduction. The lesson is rather that relatedness is only one term in the calculation. Ecological conditions that make independent breeding hopeless, such as a scarce nest site that must be defended or food that only a group can gather, raise the benefit of helping and can tip the balance even where relatedness is modest. The special feature of inheritance made a good story, but the more durable explanation lies in the interaction of kinship with circumstance.

Until late in the twentieth century, many biologists expected that animals as different as a fly and a mouse would be built by largely different sets of genes. Their bodies seemed to have little in common: one has a hard external skeleton, six jointed legs and compound eyes, the other an internal skeleton, four limbs and eyes with a single lens. If form reflects the genes that make it, the reasoning went, such different forms must be made by different genes. What came instead was one of the great surprises of modern biology. A small kit of genes that direct development, deciding where the head goes, where limbs sprout and which segments become which, turned out to be shared, in recognisable versions, across almost the entire animal kingdom.

The most striking demonstrations concerned eyes. Eyes had long been thought to have evolved independently many times, so different are the eyes of insects, squid and vertebrates. Yet a gene needed for eye development in mice has a counterpart in flies, and when the mouse version is switched on in the wrong tissue of a developing fly, the fly grows extra eyes, fly eyes, on its legs or wings. The gene does not carry a blueprint of an eye. It acts more like a switch that says, in effect, 'build an eye here', leaving the details of construction to the many other genes that each lineage has in its own repertoire.

It is tempting to conclude that the evolution of form is mostly a matter of rewiring old switches rather than inventing new parts, and there is much truth in that. Changes in when and where toolkit genes are turned on can alter the number of segments, the length of a limb or the pattern on a wing without any change in the proteins the genes make. Because a single toolkit gene acts in many tissues, a change to the gene itself would tend to disrupt many structures at once, whereas a change to one of its switches can alter a single structure and leave the rest alone. This helps explain why so much evolutionary change appears to lie in the regulatory regions of the genome.

Yet the shared toolkit should not be mistaken for a shared plan. That the same switch triggers eye-building in a fly and a mouse does not mean that their eyes are, in any useful sense, the same organ; the structures downstream of the switch were assembled separately, over hundreds of millions of years, from different materials. Common genes reveal a deep common ancestry of the control system, not a common design for what is controlled. The older view was wrong to assume that different forms require different genes; the newer enthusiasm risks the opposite error, of assuming that similar genes imply similar forms.

We tend to read the history of life as a long tournament in which the better designed win. On this view a lineage that disappeared must have had some flaw, and the dinosaurs, the textbook losers, are pictured as sluggish and outmoded long before their end. The picture is comfortable because it makes the present inhabitants of the earth look like deserving champions. For the most dramatic episodes in the record it is also misleading. During ordinary stretches of geological time, extinction does seem to track the qualities that adaptation produces: species with narrow diets, small ranges or slow breeding are more likely to vanish when conditions shift, and their places are taken by competitors that cope better. The great mass extinctions, in which a large fraction of species disappeared within a geologically brief interval, seem to follow different rules.

Consider the end of the Cretaceous period, when an asteroid struck the earth and the non-avian dinosaurs vanished along with many other groups. What determined survival in the aftermath was not, as far as can be told, excellence at the ways of life that had served well for millions of years. It was the possession of traits that happened to matter in a sudden catastrophe: small body size, a varied diet, the ability to shelter or to wait out a period of darkness and scarcity in a dormant state. Such traits had not been selected for their value in a catastrophe of this kind, which no living lineage had experienced. They were, from the point of view of the crisis, accidents.

The consequence is that mass extinctions can redirect the history of life in ways that ordinary selection would not. A group that had been flourishing, and that would probably have continued to flourish under normal conditions, can be swept away, while a group that had been marginal for tens of millions of years inherits an emptied world. The mammals, which had lived in the shadow of the dinosaurs for a very long time, are the familiar example. Their later success says little about whether they were superior to the non-avian dinosaurs in any absolute sense; it says that they passed through a narrow gate that the dinosaurs, for reasons unrelated to their earlier success, did not.

This does not mean that mass extinctions are pure lotteries. Survival in them is selective; it simply selects for different things. Nor does it mean that adaptation is unimportant, since the survivors went on to diversify through ordinary selection once the crisis had passed. What it does mean is that the long-run shape of life's history cannot be read as a straightforward measure of design quality. To understand why some groups are here and others are not, we need to know not only how well they fitted their world but also which rare disasters that world happened to contain.

Debates about the origin of life have long been organised around a chicken-and-egg problem. In every living cell today, the information needed to build proteins is stored in nucleic acids, while the work of copying those nucleic acids is done by proteins. Neither seems able to arise without the other. One family of proposals breaks the circle by giving priority to the molecule that carries information: life began, on this view, when some molecule capable of copying itself appeared, however clumsily, and natural selection among its variants did the rest. A rival family gives priority to chemistry that sustains itself: life began as networks of reactions, perhaps on mineral surfaces near hot springs on the sea floor, that captured energy from their surroundings and kept themselves going long before any of their components stored heritable information.

The case for putting the replicator first gained great force from a discovery about RNA, a close chemical relative of DNA. RNA, it turned out, can not only carry information but also catalyse reactions, and the core of the machinery that assembles proteins in every cell is itself made of RNA. A world in which RNA did both jobs, storing instructions and doing chemical work, would dissolve the chicken-and-egg problem, and that protein-building machinery looks very much like a relic of such a world. Replicator theorists add a deeper argument. Without heredity, they say, there is no evolution in any serious sense: a chemical network that merely persists cannot accumulate improvements, because there is nothing to record which variants did better.

Metabolism-first theorists reply that the RNA world has a chemistry problem it has never solved. The building blocks of RNA are intricate molecules, hard to make without the help of enzymes and prone to fall apart in water; assembling them into long chains that can copy themselves requires a degree of order that seems unlikely to arise unaided. Self-sustaining reaction networks, by contrast, need only simple ingredients and a source of energy. These theorists also argue that heredity need not wait for a dedicated molecule. A network in which certain reactions reinforce one another could persist in several alternative states, and if fragments that break away from a network tend to restart the same state, something like inheritance exists before any gene.

It is worth noticing that the two camps are often answering different questions. Replicator theorists ask what made open-ended evolution possible, and their answer, a molecule that copies information with modest accuracy, is hard to dispute as an account of that threshold. Metabolism theorists ask where the materials and energy for any such molecule came from, and their answer, a geochemical setting that drives reactions without the help of life, is equally hard to dispute as an account of the prior stage. Presented as rivals, each side scores easy points against the other's weakest claim: that replicators could assemble themselves in a chemical vacuum, or that self-maintaining networks could evolve indefinitely without anything like genes.

The more promising view, then, treats the problem as one of sequence rather than of priority in the abstract. A chemistry able to sustain itself may have been the setting in which the first replicators formed and on which they depended, and the replicators may in turn have captured and refined the network that fed them. This does not settle the matter, since the details of each stage remain obscure and may never be recovered from a planet whose earliest rocks have been largely destroyed. But it changes what counts as progress. The question is no longer which came first, as though one of them had to be the true beginning, but how a world of self-maintaining chemistry could have given rise to one in which information was inherited.

A predator that keeps a grazer in check, allowing plants to flourish, is the classic case, and the concept has proved enormously useful in persuading the public and governments that the fate of a single species can matter to a whole landscape.

The vividness of the metaphor, however, carries assumptions that ecology does not always support. An arch has one keystone, and its role is fixed by the geometry of the structure. Ecological communities are not like this. The same species may have a dominant influence in one place and a minor one in another, depending on what else lives there, on the climate and on the history of the site. A predator that controls its prey on a rocky shore where that prey has few other enemies may matter much less where the prey is also eaten by several other animals. Keystone status, in other words, is a property of a relationship in a particular setting, not a fixed attribute of a species.

The metaphor also invites a search for the one species that holds everything together, when in many communities influence is spread across several species, each of moderate importance, whose combined effects are what keep the system stable. A conservation strategy built around identifying and protecting keystones may then direct attention to a few dramatic interactions and neglect the diffuse ones, which are harder to demonstrate but not necessarily less important. The absence of a striking keystone in a community does not show that its members are interchangeable.

None of this means the concept should be discarded. Some species really do have outsized and well-documented effects, and naming them helps in arguing for their protection. The danger lies in treating the label as a discovery about a species' nature rather than a finding about a particular system, and in assuming that where no keystone has been identified, no species is indispensable. The architectural image is best used as what it is, a way of making one kind of ecological fact memorable, and not as a model of how communities are generally built.

Energy is neither created nor destroyed; momentum lost by one body is gained by another; a spinning skater who draws in her arms turns faster because her angular momentum cannot change. Presented in this way, the laws look like the deepest facts we possess, discovered by patient measurement and accepted because nature has never been caught breaking them. What Noether showed is that, for any system whose laws can be written in a certain standard mathematical form, every continuous symmetry of those laws corresponds to a quantity that is conserved. If the laws are the same today as they were yesterday, energy is conserved. If they are the same here as in the next room, momentum is conserved. If they do not depend on which way a system happens to be facing, angular momentum is conserved. The conservation laws, in other words, are not separate facts standing side by side; they are consequences of a single kind of fact, namely that the rules of nature do not care when, where or in what orientation they are applied.

It is tempting to conclude that conservation has thereby been proved, that energy must be conserved as a matter of logic. That would be a mistake. The theorem converts one question into another; it does not settle either. Whether the laws really are the same at all times is something only observation can tell us, and the theorem ensures that the evidence for the symmetry and the evidence for the conservation law are, in effect, the same evidence. What has changed is the direction of explanation. We no longer say that energy is conserved and leave the matter there; we say that energy is conserved because time is uniform, and we are then free to ask whether time is uniform.

That question has teeth. On the scale of the whole universe, which is expanding and so does not look the same from one epoch to the next, the symmetry behind energy conservation is not obviously present, and it is far from clear that a universal law of energy conservation can even be stated there. On the older view this would look like a scandal, a crack in the bedrock. On the view the theorem encourages, it is just what one should expect: where the symmetry fails, the conservation law loses its footing, and the surprise would be if it did not. A principle that once seemed beyond question has become something more useful, a claim whose limits can be foreseen.

Quantum theory is among the most precisely confirmed bodies of physics ever constructed, and it is also, by the admission of many who use it, a theory that does not quite say what it is about. The difficulty is usually called the measurement problem. The theory's central equation describes a system by a mathematical object, the wave function, which evolves smoothly and predictably for as long as nobody looks. Under that evolution a particle can come to be in a combination, or superposition, of possibilities: here and there, spinning one way and the other. Yet whenever an experimenter checks, the apparatus records exactly one result. The textbook rule bridges the gap by decree: at the moment of measurement the wave function 'collapses' to one of the possibilities, with probabilities that the theory supplies.

The trouble is that the rule invokes a notion the theory never defines. A measuring device is made of atoms, and atoms obey the smooth equation; treated as part of the physical system, the device should itself enter a superposition of having recorded each result, rather than recording one. Somewhere between the particle and the experimenter's notebook the smooth evolution is supposed to give way to the abrupt one, but the theory does not say where, or why, or what counts as a measurement in the first place. In practice nothing hangs on the answer, because the boundary can be drawn anywhere convenient without altering the predicted statistics. That is precisely what makes the problem so easy to ignore.

The proposed solutions divide, roughly, by what they are prepared to give up. Some modify the smooth equation, adding a tiny spontaneous tendency to collapse that is negligible for single particles but overwhelming for anything as large as a pointer. Others leave the equation untouched and deny that collapse ever happens: every possible outcome is realised, each in a branch of reality that cannot interact with the others, and the appearance of a single result is simply what being in one branch is like. Still others add a further ingredient to the wave function, definite positions for particles at all times, guided by the wave, so that the outcome was settled all along. A widely discussed physical process, in which a system's entanglement with its surroundings rapidly suppresses the signs of superposition, is often offered as a solution in its own right; it explains why superpositions of large objects are never seen, but on most accounts it does not explain why one outcome occurs rather than another.

It is a gap in the statement of the physics itself, one that the theory's very success has made easy to step around.

Physicists have long remarked that several of the numbers fixing the behaviour of matter seem oddly well suited to the existence of anything as complicated as a living thing. Were certain forces a little stronger or a little weaker, the argument runs, stars would burn out too quickly or never ignite, atoms heavier than hydrogen would be scarce, and chemistry would offer nothing as intricate as a cell. How tightly these numbers are really constrained is debated, and some claims of extreme precision have not survived scrutiny. Enough of the pattern has held up, however, to pose a question: why should the constants of nature fall within the narrow ranges that permit observers to exist at all?

One family of answers appeals to what is usually called anthropic reasoning. Its modest form makes a point that sounds almost too obvious to state: whatever we observe must be compatible with our being there to observe it. We should not be astonished to find ourselves on a planet with liquid water and a tolerable climate, since observers could hardly have arisen anywhere else; to treat our surroundings as a fair sample of the cosmos would be like concluding that a town's residents are unusually fit because everyone one has met there was met at the swimming pool. In this form the principle is not a theory of anything. It is a warning about bias, and as a warning it is both correct and useful.

The difficulty begins when the same reasoning is asked to explain the constants themselves. Consider a lottery. A winner who learns that millions of tickets were sold need suspect nothing: someone had to win, and the winner is simply whoever did. But if only one ticket was ever printed and it won, the winner's existence explains nothing about why that ticket was drawn; it guarantees only that, had it not been, there would be no winner left to wonder. A selection effect does explanatory work only when there are many draws among which selection can operate. Defenders of the approach reply that such an ensemble is not invented for the purpose. Some cosmological theories, developed to address quite different problems, appear to imply a vast number of regions with differing properties, and if those theories are right, the anthropic explanation comes, so to speak, free of charge. The reply has force, but it shifts the burden rather than lifting it. The explanation is now exactly as good as the theory that supplies the ensemble, and since the other regions are, on most versions, unobservable in principle, the evidence for that theory must come from somewhere else. Anthropic reasoning cannot certify its own premise.

None of this shows that the approach is unscientific, a charge often levelled against it with more heat than precision. Selection effects are a respectable part of reasoning in every empirical science, and ignoring them produces errors as surely as overrating them does. The narrower lesson is that the modest principle and the ambitious one are different claims and should not borrow each other's credibility. The first is nearly beyond dispute and explains almost nothing on its own; the second would explain a great deal, but only on a condition that the principle itself is powerless to establish.

It tells us that among patients like those enrolled, the people given a treatment did better on average than the people who were not, by an amount that can be estimated with known uncertainty. The doctor in a consulting room faces a different question, about a single person, and the gap between the two questions has produced two opposite errors, each of which presents itself as wisdom.

The first error is to apply the average as though it were a fact about each patient. An average is a summary of a distribution, and very different distributions share the same summary. Even afterwards the matter stays hidden: a patient who takes the drug and has no stroke cannot tell whether the drug or good fortune spared him. To promise a particular patient a reduction of three points is to claim knowledge the trial does not contain.

The second error starts from this observation and draws the wrong conclusion. Critics later gave this picture a name, the deficit model, and the name stuck because it captured what was wrong with it: it treated the public as a vessel with a hole in it, defined entirely by what it lacked.

The model fails first on its own terms. If resistance came from ignorance, those who knew most about a contested subject would be the most accepting of the expert view, yet it is easy to find people who have read widely on a topic and still reject the consensus, sometimes with more conviction than before they began. Knowledge, it turns out, can be recruited to defend a position as readily as to change it. A person who already distrusts the source of a claim does not experience new information as enlightenment; she experiences it as further pressure from that source. The facts are heard, but they are heard as moves in a contest whose sides were chosen before the conversation began.

The deeper failure concerns what the model leaves out. Consider a village told by a team of engineers that a new dam upstream poses no danger. The villagers may follow the hydrology perfectly well. What they also know, and what the engineers' presentation omits, is that the last assurance they received from officials turned out to be wrong, that the benefits of the dam will flow to a distant city, and that if anything goes wrong it is they, not the engineers, who will bear the loss. Their scepticism is not a vacancy waiting to be filled. It is a judgement about whom to trust and who pays, and it may be quite rational even when the hydrology is sound.

None of this means that facts are irrelevant or that every misunderstanding is secretly a stance about power. Some people do hold mistaken beliefs simply because nobody has explained a matter clearly, and for them a good explanation is exactly what is needed. The error of the deficit model was to make this the whole story. Communication that begins by asking what an audience already knows, fears and has experienced will sometimes end in explanation. But it will at least explain the right thing, and it will treat the people it addresses as parties to a decision rather than as containers for information.

Generations of students have learned that science proceeds by a method, and that the method has steps. One observes, forms a hypothesis, designs an experiment, tests the hypothesis and draws a conclusion, after which the cycle begins again. The sequence is tidy, easy to examine and, as a description of what scientists actually do, very nearly useless. It is not that no scientist has ever followed it. It is that the sciences differ so widely in what they are able to do that any single recipe either excludes much of their work or becomes so general that it no longer guides anyone.

Consider the range. An astronomer studying a distant star cannot intervene in it; she can only watch, and her ingenuity lies in finding natural cases that differ in just the respect she wants to compare. A geologist reconstructing how a mountain range formed is not testing a hypothesis by experiment but assembling a history from traces, much as a detective does, and her standard of success is the coherence of a narrative that accounts for scattered evidence. A laboratory chemist may indeed run controlled experiments, but she spends much of her time on something the textbook sequence ignores entirely: making instruments and procedures work at all. To call all of this 'the scientific method' is to give one name to several distinct crafts.

The tidy sequence is not merely inaccurate; it does some damage. By making the controlled experiment the model of all good science, it encourages the view that sciences which cannot experiment are somehow lesser, as though reconstructing the history of the Earth were a provisional activity awaiting a proper laboratory. And by presenting science as a machine that turns hypotheses into conclusions, it leaves the public poorly prepared for the ordinary untidiness of research, in which results conflict, estimates are revised and experts disagree for long periods before they converge. People taught to expect the machine are apt to read each revision as a malfunction.

Is there, then, nothing that the sciences share? There is something, but it is not a procedure. What unites the astronomer, the geologist and the chemist is a set of habits about how claims are treated once they have been made: they are exposed to colleagues who are expected to attack them, they are stated in a form that evidence could tell against, and the people who make them are expected to give them up when such evidence arrives. These are norms of a community rather than steps for an individual, and that is precisely why they cannot be captured in a flowchart. A student who had absorbed them would understand science better than one who had memorised the steps, even if she could not say in what order they come.

When two researchers, one celebrated and one unknown, publish a discovery together, the discovery is very likely to be remembered as the celebrated researcher's. The same pattern appears when two people arrive at a result independently: the one who is already eminent tends to receive the larger share of the attention, the citations and the invitations that follow. Sociologists of science named this the Matthew effect, after the verse in the Gospel of Matthew about those who have being given more, and it is usually discussed as a problem of fairness. The unknown co-author did much of the work, and someone else collected the credit.

That description is correct within its limits, but it understates what is at stake, and it also misses why the effect is so persistent. Scientists face a literature far larger than anyone could read in a lifetime. A familiar name on a paper is a cheap signal that the paper is probably worth the reader's time, and in a world of scarce attention, readers who rely on such signals are not being foolish. To some degree, then, the effect is the by-product of a reasonable strategy for coping with abundance. Condemning it as mere snobbery does not explain why careful and fair-minded people take part in it every day.

The trouble begins when attention is converted into resources. A researcher whose papers are widely read finds it easier to win grants, which pay for students and equipment, which produce more papers, which are read more widely still. Advantage compounds. After a few rounds, the difference between two careers may bear little relation to any difference in the quality of their first ideas, and the field as a whole has placed a heavy bet on one line of work for reasons that are partly accidental. Ideas that happened to originate with the less visible researcher may wait years for a hearing, or never receive one, not because they were tested and found wanting but because nobody with the means to test them was looking in their direction.

Seen in this light, the effect is less an injustice done to individuals than a distortion in how a field allocates its bets, and the remedy is not to forbid readers from noticing names, which would be both futile and costly. It is to loosen the link between attention and money: to judge some grant proposals without knowing who wrote them, to set aside funds for researchers early in their careers, and to treat a famous name as a reason to read a paper rather than as a reason to believe it.

Every year, research funding agencies ask panels of scientists to read hundreds of proposals and rank them, and every year the money runs out long before the list does. The proposals near the bottom are usually easy to set aside: they are poorly argued, or unoriginal, or plainly unworkable. The difficulty lies higher up. Among the proposals that every member of the panel agrees are good, the panel must still decide which will be funded and which will not, and the line it draws often separates projects that no one present could distinguish with any confidence. It is in response to this predicament that a few agencies have begun to experiment with a once unthinkable idea: setting a threshold of quality and then choosing among the proposals above it by lot.

The proposal is usually defended on grounds of efficiency. Ranking is laborious; panels spend weeks producing distinctions that a lottery would produce in minutes. This is true, but it is the weakest part of the case, and resting on it invites the reply that good decisions are worth what they cost. The stronger argument is about honesty. Experience suggests that two independent panels given the same proposals will often disagree about which of them fall just above the funding line and which just below, a sign that, near the top, the ranking reflects the composition of the panel at least as much as the merits of the work. A ranking of this kind already contains a large element of chance. What it lacks is any acknowledgement of the fact, and so its outcomes are experienced, by winners and losers alike, as verdicts on quality.

Hidden chance is not neutral chance. When the members of a panel cannot tell good proposals apart on their merits, other preferences fill the gap: a taste for methods they themselves use, a wariness of projects whose payoff is uncertain, a familiarity with applicants they have met. Each of these is a mild bias, and none need be deliberate, but together they tilt the funding line toward the safe and the recognisable. A lottery among the proposals above the threshold removes this tilt at the margin. It cannot make the panel bolder, but it can stop the panel's caution from deciding cases that its judgement cannot decide.

The most serious objection is not that lotteries abandon judgement, since judgement is exercised in full at the threshold, where panels are reasonably good at separating the sound from the unsound. It is that scientists need to believe that reward follows merit, and that a lottery, by announcing that it sometimes does not, will corrode the motivation on which research depends. This deserves to be taken seriously. Yet the belief it seeks to protect is, at the margin, already false, and a system that preserves morale by concealing how it works purchases that morale at the cost of misleading the very people it rewards. A researcher whose proposal loses a draw has at least been told the truth: that her work was judged fundable and that the money ran out.

Where proposals differ in ways that can be measured, as when an agency must choose a single design for a costly instrument, ranking is the right tool and chance would be an abdication. The argument applies only where judgement has reached its limit. Its real lesson concerns not lotteries but the pretence of precision, and agencies that decline to draw lots might still learn from it, for instance by publishing how close the decisions near their funding line actually were.

A scientific paper presents itself as a complete set of instructions. Its methods section lists the materials, the quantities, the temperatures and the timings, and the implicit promise is that anyone competent who follows them will see what the authors saw. This promise underwrites one of the most cherished ideas about science: that its knowledge is public, available in principle to anyone who can read, and independent of the particular people who first produced it. When a laboratory fails to reproduce a published result, the natural suspicion therefore falls either on the original claim or on the care of the people who tried to repeat it.

Anyone who has worked at a laboratory bench knows that the promise is only partly kept. Many techniques depend on skills that their practitioners cannot fully articulate: how firmly to tap a tube, how long a mixture should look cloudy before it is ready, which faint smudge on an image is signal and which is dirt. Such knowledge is not secret, and it is not mystical. It is simply of a kind that is acquired by doing and by watching others do, in the way that one learns to ride a bicycle rather than the way one learns a telephone number. A methods section can point toward it, but it cannot transmit it, any more than a manual can make its reader a competent cyclist.

It is not unusual for a group trying to build a new kind of instrument from published descriptions alone to fail again and again, while another group that has sent a member to spend some weeks in the original laboratory, or has received a visitor from it, succeeds, often without being able to say afterwards what the crucial difference was. In such cases the written instructions were not wrong. They were incomplete in ways that neither their authors nor their readers could have specified in advance, because the authors did not know which of the many things they did without thinking were the things that mattered.

Recognising this does not license complacency about how methods are reported. A great deal that is now left out of papers could be written down, and the habit of omitting it, whether to save space or to protect an advantage, deserves the criticism it receives. But the recognition does alter what we should expect from reform. Demanding ever longer methods sections will capture the explicit portion of a technique more fully while leaving the tacit portion exactly where it was. If the goal is that results can be checked by people other than those who produced them, some of the effort now spent on documents might be better spent on movement: on funding researchers to visit one another's laboratories, on recording procedures as they are actually performed, and on treating the training of a replicator as part of the cost of a replication.

There is a larger consequence as well. The picture of scientific knowledge as a body of public statements, detachable from the people who hold it, turns out to be an idealisation. Much of what a field knows lives in the hands of its practitioners and moves only when they do. This is not a reason to distrust that knowledge, since skills can be tested by their results as surely as statements can. It is a reason to treat the communities that carry it, and the slow work of passing it on, as part of the infrastructure of science rather than as a private matter of training.
