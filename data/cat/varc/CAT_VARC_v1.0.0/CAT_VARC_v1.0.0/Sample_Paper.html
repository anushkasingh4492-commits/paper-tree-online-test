Passage 1 · questions 1 to 4

When a global fast-food chain enters a new country, the first thing commentators examine is its menu. If the chain serves the same burger it serves at home, critics see cultural flattening, the erosion of local tastes by a single corporate palate. If it introduces a spiced vegetable patty or a rice dish for the local market, its defenders announce that globalisation, far from erasing difference, accommodates it. Both sides agree, without ever saying so, that the menu is where the question of cultural influence will be settled.

That assumption deserves more scrutiny than it usually receives. A menu is the part of a chain that is easiest to change and, for that very reason, the part that tells us least. What the chain actually exports is a format: a way of organising food, labour and time. The counter at which one orders without a waiter, the fixed price displayed in advance, the promise that the meal will taste the same today as it did last month, the kitchen run like an assembly line by staff who can be trained in days: these travel intact, and they carry assumptions about efficiency and predictability that are far more foreign to many food cultures than any particular ingredient. A spiced patty served through such a system is a local flavour in a global grammar.

Yet it would be wrong to infer that the format simply imposes its meanings wherever it goes, for customers bring meanings of their own. In some places a visit to a chain restaurant has been, at least for a time, a special occasion rather than a convenience: a clean, cool and respectable room in which a family can celebrate a birthday, or two young people can meet without attracting comment. The format was designed for speed, but the customers linger. They take a system built to minimise the time spent at the table and turn it into a place for spending time.

The interesting story, then, has three layers rather than one. The format is global and changes slowly; the meanings attached to it are local and can diverge sharply from what its designers intended; the menu sits between them, adjusted constantly in response to both. To read cultural influence from the menu alone is like judging the spread of a language by the loanwords it has picked up, while ignoring both its grammar and the uses to which its new speakers put it.

Passage 2 · questions 5 to 8

When two researchers, one celebrated and one unknown, publish a discovery together, the discovery is very likely to be remembered as the celebrated researcher's. The same pattern appears when two people arrive at a result independently: the one who is already eminent tends to receive the larger share of the attention, the citations and the invitations that follow. Sociologists of science named this the Matthew effect, after the verse in the Gospel of Matthew about those who have being given more, and it is usually discussed as a problem of fairness. The unknown co-author did much of the work, and someone else collected the credit.

That description is correct within its limits, but it understates what is at stake, and it also misses why the effect is so persistent. Scientists face a literature far larger than anyone could read in a lifetime. A familiar name on a paper is a cheap signal that the paper is probably worth the reader's time, and in a world of scarce attention, readers who rely on such signals are not being foolish. To some degree, then, the effect is the by-product of a reasonable strategy for coping with abundance. Condemning it as mere snobbery does not explain why careful and fair-minded people take part in it every day.

The trouble begins when attention is converted into resources. A researcher whose papers are widely read finds it easier to win grants, which pay for students and equipment, which produce more papers, which are read more widely still. Advantage compounds. After a few rounds, the difference between two careers may bear little relation to any difference in the quality of their first ideas, and the field as a whole has placed a heavy bet on one line of work for reasons that are partly accidental. Ideas that happened to originate with the less visible researcher may wait years for a hearing, or never receive one, not because they were tested and found wanting but because nobody with the means to test them was looking in their direction.

Seen in this light, the effect is less an injustice done to individuals than a distortion in how a field allocates its bets, and the remedy is not to forbid readers from noticing names, which would be both futile and costly. It is to loosen the link between attention and money: to judge some grant proposals without knowing who wrote them, to set aside funds for researchers early in their careers, and to treat a famous name as a reason to read a paper rather than as a reason to believe it.

Passage 3 · questions 9 to 12

The handbook of Epictetus's teachings opens with a distinction that has outlived nearly everything else in ancient ethics: some things are up to us and some are not. Our judgements, intentions and desires are up to us; our bodies, our possessions, our reputations and the offices we hold are not. In its modern popular form the distinction is offered as a technique for calm. Worry, it is said, comes from caring about what one cannot change, so the remedy is to stop caring about it, and the person who masters this will be undisturbed by traffic, criticism and bad news alike.

This reading is not exactly wrong, but it inverts the order of the original argument. For the Stoics, tranquillity was a consequence, not the goal. The goal was to place one's good where it actually lay, in the quality of one's own choosing, and the distinction between what is and is not up to us was a claim about value before it was advice about feeling. To say that a friend's illness is not up to me is not to say that I should be indifferent to it; it is to say that my goodness as a person does not depend on whether she recovers, but on whether I act well toward her. The Stoic was expected to visit the sick, serve in public life and keep his promises with full seriousness, while declining to stake his worth on how any of it turned out.

Read this way, the doctrine is more demanding than the popular version, not less. It asks for constant attention to one's own judgements, which the Stoics thought were the real source of most distress, and it offers no permission to withdraw. Someone who uses it to stop voting because elections are not up to him has learned a convenient half of the lesson and discarded the half that mattered.

There is, however, a genuine difficulty that the more careful reading does not remove. The line between what is up to us and what is not is drawn more sharply in the doctrine than in life. Whether I pass an examination is not wholly up to me, but it is hardly independent of my effort; whether my child grows up kind is partly my doing and partly not. Most of what we care about lies in this middle region, and a doctrine that sorts everything into two bins must either treat such outcomes as indifferent, which seems false to how they matter, or admit that our worth is, after all, partly hostage to fortune. The Stoics chose the first answer. One can admire the clarity of that choice and still doubt that it describes a life anyone actually leads.

Passage 4 · questions 13 to 16

Translators of the great books of the past often remark on a puzzle. The original of an old epic or a classical tragedy does not seem to age; readers return to it century after century and find it as alive as ever. Yet translations of the same work date quickly. A version that seemed fresh to its first readers can sound stiff or quaint within a lifetime, and every generation or two a new one appears, prefaced by the claim that its predecessors have become unreadable. Why should the copy age when the original, which is older and in many ways stranger, does not? The question is worth more attention than it usually receives, because the answer says something about what a translation is.

The usual answer is linguistic: a translation is written in the language of its own moment, with that moment's idioms and its notions of what sounds dignified and what sounds plain, and these change. The original, by contrast, has been absorbed into a tradition that reads it as a historical text and forgives its strangeness. There is truth in this, but it cannot be the whole story, since old translations are often perfectly intelligible and still feel inadequate.

A deeper reason lies in what translation requires. A great work is full of places where its sense is undecided: a word that could mean anger or grief, a line whose tone could be mocking or tender, a scene whose moral weight is left to the reader. The original can leave such places open, and much of its power to sustain rereading comes from that openness; a translator has no such freedom, because writing in another language forces choices at every point, and each choice settles a question the original left unsettled. The choices are not arbitrary, but they are shaped by what the translator's own age finds plausible or important, and so the translation records one generation's reading of the work. When the concerns of that generation fade, its translation comes to look like what it always was: an interpretation.

Seen in this light, retranslation is not a sign that earlier translators failed. It is one of the ways a classic stays alive, each version making a fresh set of decisions and so showing a new reader something the others concealed. There is even an occasional reversal. Some translations become works of literature in their own right, read not as windows onto the original but for their own sake, and these, curiously, stop ageing in the way translations usually do; they come to be read, like originals, as historical texts. Their choices no longer look like a period's reading of another book; they look like the permanent features of a book of their own.
